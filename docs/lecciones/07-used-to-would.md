## 1. Goal

Today you learn how English talks about past habits and past situations that are no
longer true: *used to* + verb, *would* + verb, and the different structure *be used to*
+ *-ing* ("estar acostumbrada a"). It connects to lesson 04: there you saw that the past
continuous is for an action in progress, not for a habit. This lesson gives you the forms
English does use for habits, and fixes four mistakes that come straight from Spanish:
*"I use to..."*, *"I would live in..."*, *"I'm used to wake up..."* and *"I didn't used
to..."*.

## 2. Words you need

Technical terms you'll see in this lesson, explained before you need them:

- **Habit (past habit)**: something you did again and again, regularly, over a period of
  time (*I used to swim every Thursday*). It matters because English has special forms
  for past habits, and Spanish does not (see section 5).
- **Action verb**: a verb for something that happens or that you do: *play*, *go*,
  *read*, *wake up*. It matters because an action can happen again and again, so it can
  be a habit with *used to* or *would*.
- **State verb**: a verb for something that simply is or lasts, without "happening":
  *live* (as your situation), *know*, *like*, *have* (own), *be*. It matters because a
  state doesn't repeat like an event, and that's why *would* rejects it.
- **Base verb (base form)**: the verb with nothing added: *play*, *live*, *be*. It
  matters because after *used to* and after *would* the verb always stays in this form.
- **Modal verb**: one of a small, closed group of helper verbs (*can, could, will,
  would, should*...) that go before a base verb and add a meaning to it. *Would* is one.
  It exists so English can add meanings like habit, condition or possibility without
  changing the main verb.
- **Auxiliary verb**: a helper verb that carries tense, questions and negatives. In this
  lesson the important one is *did*: it carries the past in *"Did you use to...?"* and
  *"I didn't use to..."*.
- **Past continuous**: *was/were* + verb-*ing* (*I was cooking*), from lesson 04: an
  action in progress at one moment in the past. It matters because Spanish speakers try
  to use it for habits, and English doesn't.
- **Imperfecto**: the Spanish past form *jugaba*, *vivía*, *leía*. It covers two jobs at
  once: an action in progress and a habit. It matters because it explains why Spanish
  doesn't need separate habit forms.
- **Preposition**: a small word that links a noun to the rest of the sentence: *to*,
  *at*, *in*, *about*. It matters because in *be used to*, the word *to* is a
  preposition, and that's why *-ing* follows it.
- **Gerund (-ing form)**: a verb + *-ing* that works like a noun: *waking up*,
  *reading*. It exists so a verb can go in a place where English needs a noun, for
  example after a preposition.
- **Calque**: a word-by-word copy of a structure from your own language that doesn't
  exist in the new one. It matters because *"I use to wake up early"* is a calque of
  *"suelo madrugar"*.

## 3. What it's for (uses)

These forms cover four jobs:

- **Past habits that stopped** (actions): *I used to swim every Thursday.* (I don't
  anymore.)
- **Past states or situations that are no longer true**: *I used to live in Cali.* /
  *This café used to be a bookshop.* Only *used to* can do this job, not *would*.
- **Repeated actions inside a story**, once the time is already set: *When we were kids,
  my grandmother would tell us stories every night.* This is more common in storytelling
  and a little more formal.
- **Being accustomed to something** (a different structure): *I'm used to working
  late.* / *I'm getting used to the cold.* This one works in any tense, including the
  present.

*used to*, no *would*; acciones repetidas dentro de una historia, cuando el momento ya

  🇬🇧 *I usually read before bed.* (for a habit now, English uses the present simple +
  *usually*; *used to* only exists in the past)

## 4. How to build it (form)

The scene: **who → used to / would → base verb → what/where → when (back then)**. The
main verb never changes: *used to* or *would* carries the "habit in the past" idea, and
the action stays in its base form. For *be used to*, the scene is different: **who → is
(was, will be) → used to → a thing or an -ing action**.

| Form | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| *used to* (habit or state) | I used to live in Cali. | I didn't use to live in Cali. | Did you use to live in Cali? |
| *would* (repeated action, in a story) | Every Sunday we would cook together. | (rare for habits; use *didn't use to*) | (rare for habits; use *Did you use to...?*) |
| *be used to* (accustomed) | I'm used to waking up early. | I'm not used to waking up early. | Are you used to waking up early? |
| *get used to* (becoming accustomed) | I'm getting used to the cold. | I can't get used to the cold. | Did you get used to the cold? |

El verbo principal nunca cambia: *used to* o *would* carga la idea de "hábito en el
pasado", y la acción queda en su forma base. Para *be used to* la escena es distinta:

### Contraste ES → EN (forma)

- 🇪🇸 El español cambia la terminación del verbo: *jugar → jugaba*.
  🇬🇧 English keeps the verb in its base form and puts a helper in front: *play → used
  to play* / *would play*. Never *"I used to played"*.
- 🇪🇸 La pregunta usa la misma forma del verbo, solo cambia la entonación: *"¿Antes
  vivías en Cali?"*
  🇬🇧 English needs *did* in front, and then *use* goes back to its base form: *"Did you
  use to live in Cali?"* Never *"Did you used to...?"*
- 🇪🇸 *Estoy acostumbrada a madrugar.* (preposición *a* + infinitivo)
  🇬🇧 *I'm used to getting up early.* (preposition *to* + *-ing*). Never *"I'm used to
  get up early."*

### Pronunciation

In *used to* (the habit) and *be used to* (accustomed), the *s* sounds like a normal
Spanish *s*, not like a buzzing *z*. The ordinary verb *use* ("usar") and its past
*used* ("usé") do have the buzzing sound. In fast speech, *used to* often sounds like
one word, and the *d* disappears before the *t* of *to*.

- `used to (IÚS-tu)`, rápido: `(IÚS-ta)`
- `be used to (bi IÚS-tu)`
- `use, verbo "usar" (IÚZ: la z vibra como zumbido de abeja)`
- `used, pasado de "usar" (IÚZD: la z vibra como zumbido de abeja)`
- `didn't use to (DÍ-dent IÚS-tu)`

- `I'd (ÁID)`, contracción de *I would*

## 5. Why it works this way

This lesson touches two Big Ideas from `docs/ideas-clave.md`: **English marks time and
completion with small "tool verbs"** (idea 4: *would* and *did* carry the time, the main
verb stays in its base form) and **English uses a fixed, small set of modal verbs**
(idea 6: *would* is a modal, so the verb after it never changes). Here is the main Why
chain, for why English has special habit forms at all, plus four shorter causal notes
for the other questions in this lesson.

### Main Why chain: why English has special forms for past habits

**1. The big picture.** English splits the "background" past into two different jobs,
with two different forms: an action **in progress** at one moment (the past continuous,
lesson 04) and an action **repeated** over a period (*used to*, *would*). Spanish puts
both jobs into one form, the imperfecto. In English, small helper words carry that
difference, not the ending of the verb (see Big Idea 4, "tool verbs").

**2. Where it comes from.** Romance languages, Spanish included, don't have a form that
is only for habits: their imperfect combines the past with the idea of "ongoing" and
can mean habitual or continuous. English has no general imperfect: it expresses those
meanings in different ways depending on whether the event is continuous or habitual. Its
habit marker, *used to*, was built from the verb *use*, which came into English through
Old French from Latin; the Latin noun *usus* meant "use, custom, practice, habit". The
phrase *used to* ("formerly did or was") comes from an old sense of *use*, "to
accustom", and is recorded from around 1300 and common from around 1400. One early
example, from before 1387: *"Englische men used for to goo into abbayes of Fraunce"*
("English men used to go into abbeys of France").

**3. The problem it creates.** Because English has no single imperfecto, *jugaba* has
no automatic translation. If you choose the past continuous (*"I was playing in the
park"*), an English listener pictures one moment in progress, a frame with something
happening around it, and waits for the event that interrupted it (*"...when my mom
called"*). The meaning "this was my routine for years" gets lost.

**4. English's solution.** English marks the habit with its own words. *Used to* + base
verb says "this happened regularly, or was true, and it isn't anymore". *Would* + base
verb says "this happened again and again" inside a story where the time is already set.
The past simple can also work with a frequency word (*"We always ate dinner at six"*),
but it doesn't underline the repetition as clearly. In every case the main verb stays in
its base form: the helper carries the habit, as in Big Idea 4.

**5. How native speakers see it.** *A useful mental model*: the past continuous is one
paused frame from a film (one moment, with something in progress). *Used to* is the
whole photo album of a period of your life, with a line drawn under it: "that was then,
it's not now". The "no longer true" part is what the grammar sources say; the film and
the album are only a way to picture it, not a proven fact about how native speakers
think.

### Also: why *used to* works for states and actions, but *would* only for actions

**The fact** (verified): *used to* can describe a state or situation that is no longer
true (*We used to live in Manchester*); *would* can't (*not: We would live in
Manchester*). *Would* also has other jobs: the conditional (*I would go if I felt
better*) and the future seen from the past. Because of that, *would* only sounds like a
habit when the time is already clear from the context (*Last summer we would go there
every day*); *"We would go there"* alone doesn't say "habit".

**Why, as a mental model** (no source found this session for the deeper reason):
*would* works like pressing "replay" on a scene you already opened. Each replay is an
event that happens again: *she would open the window, she would make coffee*. A state
doesn't happen again and again; it just stays. So *"I would live in Cali"* has no event
to replay, and the listener hears the other job of *would*, the conditional: *"viviría
en Cali"*. *Used to* has no other job, so it can hold both states and actions.

### Also: why *used to* only exists in the past (and why *"I use to"* is a calque)

*Used to* is only found in the past tense. For habits **now**, standard English has no
special habit marker at all: it uses the present simple, often with a frequency word
(*I usually read before bed*, *I often work late*). Spanish is different: *soler* works
in the present (*suelo levantarme a las siete*) and in the imperfecto (*solía*). So when
a Spanish speaker says *"I use to wake up early"*, she is copying *"suelo madrugar"* into
a slot English leaves empty. The fix is simple: present simple + *usually*: *"I usually
wake up early."*

palabra de frecuencia (*I usually read before bed*, *I often work late*). El español es
distinto: *soler* funciona en presente (*suelo levantarme a las siete*) y en imperfecto
(*solía*). Entonces, cuando una hispanohablante dice *"I use to wake up early"*, está

### Also: why *be used to* is a different structure, and why it takes *-ing*

*Be used to* means "be accustomed to" or "be familiar with", and it works in the past,
present or future. Look at its pieces: *be* (in any tense) + *used* (here an adjective,
"accustomed", not a verb) + *to*. That *to* is a **preposition**, not the *to* of an
infinitive: the dictionary lists *be used* and *get used* among the verbs followed by
the preposition *to*, together with *look forward*. A preposition needs a noun after it
(*I'm used to the noise*, *I'm used to it*). If you want to put an action there, English
turns the verb into a noun: the *-ing* form (*I'm used to waking up early*). This fits
its history: when *use* first meant "accustomed", it was normally followed by a
preposition, usually *in* or *to*.

**Quick test:** if you can put *it* right after *to* (*I'm used to it*, *I look forward
to it*), that *to* is a preposition, and a verb after it takes *-ing*.

acción ahí, el inglés convierte el verbo en sustantivo: la forma *-ing* (*I'm used to
waking up early*). Esto encaja con su historia: cuando *use* empezó a significar
"acostumbrado", normalmente iba seguido de una preposición, casi siempre *in* o *to*.

**Prueba rápida:** si puedes poner *it* justo después de *to* (*I'm used to it*, *I look
forward to it*), ese *to* es una preposición, y un verbo después de él va en *-ing*.

</details>

### Also: why it's *didn't use to* (no -d), and why *used to* has an *s* sound

Big Idea 4 again: when *did* appears, it already carries the past, so the main verb goes
back to its base form. You write *didn't work*, not *"didn't worked"*, and in the same
way *didn't use to*, not *"didn't used to"*. Both spellings are common, but many people
consider the *-d* form incorrect, so don't use it in exams. The same goes for questions:
*Did you use to...?* In fast speech the *d* is hard to hear before *to*, so your ear
can't tell you which spelling is right; the rule about *did* can.

The sound: in *used to*, *used* is pronounced with an *s* sound, while the ordinary verb
*use* and its past *used* have a *z* sound. The pronunciation of *used to* is affected
by the *t* of *to*.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

Otra vez la idea clave 4: cuando aparece *did*, ya carga el pasado, así que el verbo
principal vuelve a su forma base. Escribes *didn't work*, no *"didn't worked"*, y de la
misma forma *didn't use to*, no *"didn't used to"*. Las dos escrituras son comunes, pero
mucha gente considera incorrecta la forma con *-d*, así que no la uses en exámenes. Lo
mismo pasa con las preguntas: *Did you use to...?* Hablando rápido, la *d* casi no se
oye antes de *to*, así que el oído no te dice cuál escritura es la correcta; la regla de
*did* sí.

El sonido: en *used to*, *used* se pronuncia con sonido de *s*, mientras que el verbo
común *use* y su pasado *used* tienen sonido de *z*. La pronunciación de *used to* está
afectada por la *t* de *to*.

</details>

## 6. When NOT to use it (traps)

- ❌ *"I use to wake up early."* → ✅ *"I usually wake up early."*
  *Why:* *used to* only exists in the past. For a habit now, use the present simple +
  *usually*. *"I use to"* is a calque of *"suelo"*.
- ❌ *"I would live in Bogotá when I was a student."* → ✅ *"I used to live in Bogotá
  when I was a student."*
  *Why:* *live* here is a state, and *would* only works for repeated actions. With a
  state, *would* sounds like the conditional (*"viviría"*).
- ❌ *"I'm used to wake up early."* → ✅ *"I'm used to waking up early."*
  *Why:* in *be used to*, *to* is a preposition, so a verb after it takes *-ing*.
- ❌ *"I didn't used to drink coffee."* → ✅ *"I didn't use to drink coffee."*
  *Why:* *did* already carries the past, so *use* goes back to its base form.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español (por qué de cada trampa)</summary>

1. *Used to* solo existe en pasado. Para un hábito de ahora, usa present simple +
   *usually*. *"I use to"* es un calco de *"suelo"*.
2. *Live* aquí es un estado, y *would* solo funciona con acciones repetidas. Con un
   estado, *would* suena a condicional (*"viviría"*).
3. En *be used to*, *to* es una preposición, así que un verbo después de él va en
   *-ing*.
4. *Did* ya carga el pasado, así que *use* vuelve a su forma base.
</details>

## 7. Hack

**The "but not anymore" test.** *Used to* always carries an invisible *"...but not
anymore"*. Say your sentence and add that tail in your head. If it fits, use *used to*
(or *would*, if it's a repeated action inside a story you already started). If it
doesn't fit because it's still true now, you're in the present: *usually* for a habit
(*I usually wake up early*), or *be used to* + *-ing* for "estoy acostumbrada" (*I'm
used to waking up early*).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

**La prueba del "pero ya no".** *Used to* siempre lleva un *"...pero ya no"* invisible.
Di tu oración y agrégale esa cola en la cabeza. Si encaja, usa *used to* (o *would*, si
es una acción repetida dentro de una historia que ya empezaste). Si no encaja porque
todavía es verdad, estás en el presente: *usually* para un hábito (*I usually wake up
early*), o *be used to* + *-ing* para "estoy acostumbrada" (*I'm used to waking up
early*).
</details>

## 9. Read

*Theme: HCI and the psychology of habits.*

Before smartphones, my father **used to** keep a paper map in the glove box of his car.
On long trips to the coast, he **would** unfold it over the steering wheel at every gas
station, and my mother **would** trace the road with her finger while he complained
about the tiny print. I **used to** think this was a family ritual, not a method.
Getting lost **didn't use to** feel like a failure; it was part of the trip.

Today I **am used to** a blue dot that shows me where I am before I even ask. I **got
used to** it so fast that I barely remember how much attention a map needed. That raises
a design question worth asking: when a tool does the thinking for us, do we stop building
our own picture of a place? My father never called himself a user, but every Sunday he
was running a small experiment in human-computer interaction: one person, one sheet of
paper, and a road that **used to** feel much longer.

## 10. Summary

1. *Used to* + base verb = a past habit or state that is no longer true; *would* + base
   verb = a repeated action inside a story, never a state.
2. For habits now, English says *usually* + present simple, never *"I use to"*; for
   "estar acostumbrada", it says *be used to* + noun or *-ing*.
3. The past continuous is not for habits: *"I used to play"*, not *"I was playing"*,
   for a routine.

<summary>Ver en español</summary>

1. *Used to* + verbo base = un hábito o estado pasado que ya no es verdad; *would* +
   verbo base = una acción repetida dentro de una historia, nunca un estado.
2. Para hábitos de ahora, el inglés dice *usually* + present simple, nunca *"I use
   to"*; para "estar acostumbrada", dice *be used to* + sustantivo o *-ing*.
3. El past continuous no es para hábitos: *"I used to play"*, no *"I was playing"*,
   para una rutina.
