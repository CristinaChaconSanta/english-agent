## 1. Goal

Today you review the **present simple** (the plain present: *I work*, *she works*) for
habits, routines, and facts. You already know the form; this lesson targets the four places
where Spanish speakers still slip at B1: forgetting the *-s* (*"She work"*), doubling it
(*"Does she works?"*), copying *estoy trabajando* for habits (*"I am work here"*), and mixing
up *"What do you do?"* with *"What are you doing?"*.

**Why now:** lesson 01 showed that *be* makes questions and negatives by itself. Every other
verb needs *do*, and that is today's lesson. The present simple is also the base you need
before the Present Perfect and before contrasting simple vs. continuous forms, and it is how
you describe routines in IELTS Speaking Part 1.

## 2. Words you need

Technical terms you'll see in this lesson, explained before you need them:

- **Present simple**: the plain present form of a verb, one word only: *work*, *works*. It
  exists to talk about what is generally true: habits, routines, and facts, not what is
  happening this second.
- **Base form**: the dictionary form of a verb, with nothing added: *work*, *go*, *have*. It
  matters because after *do*, *does*, or *did*, English always uses the base form.
- **Ending (inflection)**: letters added to the end of a word to change its grammar, like
  the *-s* in *works* or the *-ed* in *worked*. Spanish uses many endings to show who is
  acting (*trabajo*, *trabajas*, *trabaja*); English kept very few (see section 5).
- **Third person singular**: *he*, *she*, *it*, or any one person or thing that is not "I"
  or "you": *my sister*, *the app*, *Tomás*. It matters because it is the only place in the
  present simple where the verb changes shape (*she works*).
- **Auxiliary verb**: a helper verb that carries the grammar of a question or a negative so
  the main verb doesn't have to. In this lesson the auxiliary is *do* / *does*. It exists
  because in modern English a main verb cannot make a question or a negative by itself.
- **Dummy auxiliary**: *do* when it works only as grammatical support and adds no meaning of
  its own, as in *"Does she work?"*. It exists so ordinary verbs can form questions and
  negatives. It is different from *do* as a main verb meaning "hacer" (*"I do yoga"*).
- **Finite verb**: the one verb in a clause that shows time (present or past) and agrees with
  the subject: *works*, *does*, *worked*, *did*. It matters because English lets only one
  verb per clause carry that tag. When *does* arrives, *work* loses its *-s*.
- **Present continuous**: *am / is / are* + verb-*ing*: *she is working*. It exists to talk
  about something in progress right now or only for a limited time. You need it today to see
  what the present simple is NOT for.
- **Frequency adverb**: a word that says how often: *always*, *usually*, *often*,
  *sometimes*, *never*. It exists to describe habits, so it is a strong signal that you need
  the present simple.
- **Calque**: a word-for-word copy of a structure from another language, like *"I am work"*
  from *"estoy trabajando"*. Naming it helps you catch it when you hear yourself say it.
- **State verb**: a verb that describes a state, not an action: *know*, *like*, *want*,
  *understand*. It matters because state verbs use the present simple even for "right now":
  *"I know the answer,"* not *"I am knowing."* (Lesson 04 goes deeper.)

## 3. What it's for (uses)

The present simple describes the world as it **usually is**, not as it is this second:

- **Habits and routines**: *I read before bed.* / *She usually takes the metro to the
  studio.*
- **Facts and general truths**: *Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.* / *Good interfaces
  hide their complexity.*
- **Permanent situations**: *I live in Bogotá.* / *He works at a museum.*
- **States, even right now** (with state verbs): *I know what you mean.* / *She wants a new
  laptop.*

## 4. How to build it (form)

The scene: **who → does what → how often / where / when**. The verb is a fixed routine
attached to the subject, not a film running right now.

| | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / You / We / They | I work | I don't work (do not) | Do you work? |
| He / She / It | She works | She doesn't work (does not) | Does she work? |

Two things to notice in the table:

1. The *-s* appears **only** with *he / she / it* in the affirmative.
2. In negatives and questions, *do* or *does* carries the grammar, and the main verb goes
   back to its **base form**: *does*... *work*, never *works*.

**Spelling of the *-s*:**
- Most verbs: add *-s*: *work → works*, *play → plays*.
- Verbs ending in *-o, -ch, -sh, -s, -x, -z*: add *-es*: *go → goes*, *watch → watches*,
  *fix → fixes*.
- Consonant + *y*: change to *-ies*: *study → studies*, *carry → carries*.
- Irregular: *have → has*. And *be* has its own forms (*am / is / are*, lesson 01).
- Modal verbs never take *-s*: *she can*, *she must* (not *"she cans"*).

**Word order with frequency adverbs:** before the main verb, but after *be*: *She always
arrives late.* / *She is always late.*

Dos cosas de la tabla: (1) la *-s* aparece **solo** con *he / she / it* en afirmativo. (2) En

### Contraste ES → EN (forma)

- 🇪🇸 *¿Trabaja ella los sábados?* (el mismo verbo; solo cambia la entonación, o el orden)
  🇬🇧 *Does she work on Saturdays?* (a new word, *does*, appears in front, and *work* loses
  its *-s*).
- 🇪🇸 *Ella no trabaja los sábados.* (solo se agrega *no* antes del verbo)
  🇬🇧 *She doesn't work on Saturdays.* (*not* needs *does* to attach to: *"She works not"*
  is not modern English).
- 🇪🇸 *Ella siempre llega tarde.* / *Ella llega tarde siempre.* (el adverbio se puede mover)

## 5. Why it works this way

This lesson touches three Big Ideas from `docs/ideas-clave.md`: **English leans on word order,
not word endings** (idea 1), **English always needs a filled subject slot** (idea 2), and
**English marks time and completion with small "tool verbs"** (idea 4). The main Why chain
answers why the *-s* survived. Three shorter causal notes after it answer why *do/does*
appears, why the *-s* moves to *does*, and why English splits habits from "right now".

### Main Why chain: why only the 3rd person kept an ending

**1. The big picture.** English is a language that shows "who does what" mainly through word
order and a mandatory subject, not through verb endings (Big Ideas 1 and 2). A modern English
verb in the present has only two shapes: *work* and *works*. Spanish has a different ending
for almost every person: *trabajo, trabajas, trabaja, trabajamos, trabajan*.

**2. Where it comes from.** English verbs used to have an ending for each person. In Old
English, *stelan* ("to steal") was *stele*, *stilst*, *stilþ*, *stelaþ* (þ is an old letter,
written today as *th*). In Middle English the usual pattern was *-e* for "I" (*ich here*, "I
hear"), *-est* for "thou" (*þou spekest*, "thou speakest"), and *-eþ* for he/she/it (*he
comeþ*, "he comes"). The plural changed by region: the South kept *-eþ*, the Midlands used
*-en*, and the North used *-es* for the 3rd person singular and for the plural. Then each
ending fell for its own reason:
- The *-e* of "I" was a weak final vowel, and by Chaucer's time that vowel was silent in
  normal speech. *Ich here* became just *I hear*.
- The *-est* of "thou" left with its pronoun: by 1650, *thou* sounded old-fashioned, and it
  later disappeared from standard English.
- The regional plural endings (*-en*, *-eþ*, *-es*) are not part of modern standard English.
- The 3rd person had two competitors, *-eth* and *-s*. In Early Modern English, *-eth*
  became obsolete and *-s* survived. You can see both side by side in Shakespeare: *"With
  her, that hateth thee and hates us all."*

**3. The problem it creates.** After all those losses, the verb no longer tells you who is
acting, so the subject has to be said every time (Big Idea 2). The *-s* is left alone as the
only person ending in the present. It adds almost no information, because the subject already
says "one third person": in *she works*, *she* has done the job before *-s* arrives. That is
exactly why it is easy to forget: nobody misunderstands *"she work,"* but it sounds wrong
and it lowers your accuracy score.

**4. English's solution.** English keeps the *-s* as a fixed agreement tag: *he / she / it*
(or one person or thing) + verb-*s*; everyone else gets the base form. It is one small rule
with no exceptions for regular verbs, so it can be checked automatically: see a singular third
person subject in the present simple, add *-s*.

**5. How native speakers see it.** *A useful mental model* (not a sourced fact): the sources
above show *that* each other ending disappeared and that *-s* won over *-eth*; they don't give
one single proven reason why the 3rd person is the one that kept an ending. Picture the *-s*
as a fossil: the last survivor of an old system of endings. For native speakers it is
automatic, not meaningful; they notice it mostly when it is missing, the way you notice a
missing accent mark in Spanish.

revisar en automático: si ves un sujeto de tercera persona singular en present simple, agrega
*-s*.

cuando falta, como tú notas una tilde que falta en español.

### Also: why questions and negatives need *do/does*, but *be* doesn't

**Big picture → problem → solution** (Big Idea 4, tool verbs; continues lesson 01). In modern
English, *not* can only attach to an auxiliary verb or to *be*, and a question needs an
auxiliary or *be* to swap places with the subject. *Be* can do both jobs by itself: *"She is
not at home,"* *"Is she at home?"* An ordinary verb like *know* or *work* cannot: *"I know
not"* is archaic, and *"Know you him?"* is not idiomatic today. So when there is no other
auxiliary, English brings in *do*: *"I don't know,"* *"Do you know him?"* In Middle English
this *do* seems to have had a meaning of its own; by Early Modern English that meaning was
lost, which is why grammarians call it a **dummy auxiliary**: pure support, no meaning. That
is also why you never see *"Do you are ready?"*: *be* already does the job, so *do* has
nothing to support.

### Also: why the *-s* moves to *does* (*"Does she work?"*, never *"Does she works?"*)

**Big picture → problem → solution** (Big Idea 4). An English clause has only **one finite
verb**: the one that carries the time and the person tag (see "Words you need"). In *"She
works,"* that verb is *works*. When *does* comes in to build the question, *does* becomes the
finite verb, and the main verb that follows it is a bare base form that doesn't inflect. The
tag can't be in two places at once, so it jumps from *works* to *does*: *does she work?*
(not *works*). The same thing happens in the past: *"She came"* → *"Did she come?"* (not
*came*), and in emphasis: *"She does work hard."*

### Also: why English separates habits from "right now", and Spanish doesn't have to

**Big picture → problem → solution** (Big Idea 4, tool verbs). In Spanish, the plain present
covers both: the Real Academia describes a "presente progresivo" use that alternates with
*estar* + gerundio (*Me molestas* ~ *Me estás molestando con ese ruido*), next to the
"presente genérico" for habits and properties (*Madruga muchísimo*). So *¿Qué haces?* can
mean "what's your job?" or "what are you doing now?", and *estar* + gerundio is an option,
not an obligation. English went a different way: the *be* + *-ing* form (*I am walking*)
became the dominant way to express an action in progress by the end of the Early Modern
period, pushing out older options like *"I am a-walking"* and *"I do walk."* Today, English
uses the present continuous for what is happening at the moment of speaking, and the present
simple for habits, facts, and permanent situations. The step from those two facts to "so the
simple present narrowed to habits" is *a useful mental model*, not a sourced historical claim:
once English had a dedicated tool verb for "in progress", the plain present stopped doing that
job for action verbs. State verbs (*know*, *like*, *want*) are the exception: they stay in the
present simple even right now.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

**Panorama → problema → solución** (idea clave 4, verbos herramienta). En español, el presente
simple cubre las dos cosas: la Real Academia describe un uso de "presente progresivo" que
alterna con *estar* + gerundio (*Me molestas* ~ *Me estás molestando con ese ruido*), junto al
"presente genérico" para hábitos y propiedades (*Madruga muchísimo*). Por eso *¿Qué haces?*
puede significar "¿a qué te dedicas?" o "¿qué estás haciendo ahora?", y *estar* + gerundio es
una opción, no una obligación. El inglés tomó otro camino: la forma *be* + *-ing* (*I am
walking*) se volvió la forma dominante de expresar una acción en curso hacia el final del
periodo moderno temprano, desplazando opciones más viejas como *"I am a-walking"* y *"I do
walk."* Hoy, el inglés usa el present continuous para lo que está pasando en el momento en que
hablas, y el present simple para hábitos, hechos y situaciones permanentes. El paso de esos dos
hechos a "entonces el present simple se redujo a hábitos" es *un modelo mental útil*, no una
afirmación histórica con fuente: una vez que el inglés tuvo un verbo herramienta dedicado a "en
curso", el presente simple dejó de hacer ese trabajo con los verbos de acción. Los verbos de
estado (*know*, *like*, *want*) son la excepción: se quedan en present simple incluso ahora
mismo.

</details>

## 6. When NOT to use it (traps)

- ❌ *"She work in a design studio."* → ✅ *"She works in a design studio."*
  *Why:* one third person subject in the present simple always gets the *-s* tag.
- ❌ *"Does she works on weekends?"* → ✅ *"Does she work on weekends?"*
  *Why:* the tag moves to *does*; the main verb goes back to its base form.
- ❌ *"I am work at a museum."* → ✅ *"I work at a museum."*
  *Why:* a calque of *estoy trabajando*, used for a habit. For a permanent job, use the
  present simple. *"I am working at a museum"* exists, but it means a temporary situation
  (this month, this project), and *"I am work"* is never correct: after *am* you need *-ing*.
- ❌ *"What are you doing?"* (to ask about someone's job at a conference) → ✅ *"What do you
  do?"*
  *Why:* *What do you do?* asks about your job or routine; *What are you doing?* asks about
  this exact moment. Answer the first with *"I'm a UX researcher"* or *"I design
  interfaces."*

<details>
<summary>Ver en español (por qué de cada trampa)</summary>

1. Un sujeto de tercera persona singular en present simple siempre lleva la etiqueta *-s*.
2. La etiqueta se muda a *does*; el verbo principal vuelve a su forma base.
3. Es un calco de *estoy trabajando* usado para un hábito. Para un trabajo permanente, usa el
   present simple. *"I am working at a museum"* existe, pero significa una situación temporal
   (este mes, este proyecto), y *"I am work"* nunca es correcto: después de *am* necesitas
   *-ing*.
4. *What do you do?* pregunta por tu trabajo o tu rutina; *What are you doing?* pregunta por
   este momento exacto. La primera se responde con *"I'm a UX researcher"* o *"I design
   interfaces."*
</details>

## 7. Hack

**Present simple is the caption under a photo album, not the live video.** It says what the
subject does *in general* (*She paints landscapes*). If you can add "right now, as I speak",
switch to the video: *She is painting.* And one tag rule for the caption: the *-s* goes on
**one verb only**, the first one after *he / she / it*. If *does* is there, *does* takes it.
(Modals like *can* never take it.)

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

**El present simple es el pie de foto de un álbum, no el video en vivo.** Dice lo que el
sujeto hace *en general* (*She paints landscapes*). Si puedes agregar "ahora mismo, mientras
hablo", cambia al video: *She is painting.* Y una sola regla de etiqueta para el pie de foto:
la *-s* va en **un solo verbo**, el primero después de *he / she / it*. Si está *does*, *does*
se la lleva. (Los modales como *can* nunca la llevan.)
</details>

## 9. Read

*Theme: art and attention.*

Tomás **works** as a guard in a small art museum. He **doesn't paint**, and he **doesn't
have** a degree in art history, but he **knows** every room by heart. Every morning, he
**opens** the doors at nine, **checks** the lights, and **waits**.

Most visitors **follow** the same pattern. They **walk** in, **look** at the famous painting
near the entrance, **take** a photo, and **leave**. A typical visit **lasts** a few minutes.
Tomás **doesn't judge** them. People **have** busy lives, and a museum **competes** with every
screen in their pockets.

Today, though, something unusual **is happening**. A young woman **is sitting** on the floor
in front of a small, dark painting that nobody ever **notices**. She **isn't taking** photos.
She **is** just **looking**.

Tomás **wonders**: what **does** she **see** that the others **don't**? **Does** she **know**
the artist? Or **does** attention itself **change** what a painting **becomes**?

He **doesn't ask**. He simply **stands** a little farther away and **lets** her look.

*(160 words)*

<summary>Ver en español (traducción, para revisar comprensión después de leer)</summary>

Tomás trabaja como guardia en un pequeño museo de arte. No pinta y no tiene un título en

La mayoría de los visitantes siguen el mismo patrón. Entran, miran la pintura famosa cerca de

## 10. Summary

1. The present simple is for habits, routines, facts, and permanent situations; for "right
   now", use *am / is / are* + *-ing* (except with state verbs like *know*).
2. Only *he / she / it* gets the *-s*; in questions and negatives, *do / does* carries the
   grammar and the main verb goes back to its base form.
3. The biggest trap: the *-s* goes on one verb only: *"Does she work?"*, never *"Does she
   works?"*.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

1. El present simple es para hábitos, rutinas, hechos y situaciones permanentes; para "ahora
   mismo", usa *am / is / are* + *-ing* (salvo con verbos de estado como *know*).
2. Solo *he / she / it* lleva la *-s*; en preguntas y negaciones, *do / does* carga la
   gramática y el verbo principal vuelve a su forma base.
3. La trampa más grande: la *-s* va en un solo verbo: *"Does she work?"*, nunca *"Does she
   works?"*.
</details>
