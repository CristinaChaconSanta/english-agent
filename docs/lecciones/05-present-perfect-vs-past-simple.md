# Lesson 05: Present perfect simple vs past simple

*Bloque 1, unidad 2. Nivel: B1 (Core Inventory §B1 "Verb forms"). Criterio IELTS:

## 1. Goal

Today you learn to choose between two ways of talking about the past: the **past simple**
(*I saw it*) and the **present perfect** (*I have seen it*). This is a classic trap for
Spanish speakers because Spanish splits the past in a different place, so choosing well here
raises your grammatical accuracy in every IELTS Speaking and Writing task.

**Success measure:** 5 new Check items, pass with 4/5.

## 2. Words you need

Technical terms you'll see in this lesson, explained before you need them:

- **Tense**: the form of a verb that places an action in time (past, present). It exists so
  the listener knows *when* the thing you describe happens.
- **Past simple**: the one-word past form of a verb: *worked*, *saw*, *ate*. It exists to
  show a finished event at a finished time, like a photo with a date written on it.
- **Present perfect**: *have* or *has* + past participle: *have worked*, *has seen*. It
  exists to connect something that happened before now to the present moment.
- **Past participle**: the third form of a verb: *worked*, *seen*, *eaten*, *been*. It exists
  to build the perfect tenses, always after *have*.
- **Auxiliary verb**: a helper verb that carries grammar, not the main meaning. In *I have
  seen it*, *have* carries the time (present) and *seen* carries the meaning. It exists
  because English marks time with small tool verbs instead of long endings (see section 5).
- **Time expression**: a word or phrase that says when: *yesterday*, *in 2019*, *since
  March*, *this week*. It exists to anchor the event in time, and in English it often
  decides which tense you can use.
- **Finished time / unfinished time**: finished time is a period that is over (*yesterday*,
  *last week*, *in 2019*, *when I was a child*). Unfinished time is a period that still
  includes now (*today*, *this week*, *this year*, *in my life*, *so far*). This pair exists
  because English picks the tense from it.
- **Duration / starting point**: duration is how long something lasts (*three years*, *two
  hours*); starting point is when it began (*2020*, *Monday*, *last summer*). This pair
  exists because *for* goes with a duration and *since* goes with a starting point.
- **Calque**: a word-for-word copy of a structure from another language. *"I live here since
  2020"* is a calque of *"vivo aquí desde 2020"*. The label exists to help you spot errors
  that come from Spanish, not from English.
- **Contraction**: the short spoken form: *I've* (*I have*), *she's* (*she has* or *she
  is*), *haven't* (*have not*). It exists because spoken English compresses grammar words;
  careful: *she's seen it* means *she has seen it*.

  has* o *she is*), *haven't* (*have not*). Existe porque el inglés hablado comprime las
  palabras gramaticales; ojo: *she's seen it* significa *she has seen it*.

## 3. What it's for (uses)

English uses the two forms for different camera shots of the past:

- **Present perfect, life experience up to now (no date)**: *I have visited three design
  museums.* / *Have you ever been to Sweden?*
- **Present perfect, a past action with a result you can see now**: *I have lost my notes*
  (so I can't check them now).
- **Present perfect, a situation that started in the past and is still true**: *She has
  lived in Medellín since 2021.* / *We have worked together for two years.*
- **Past simple, a finished event at a finished time, or the details of a story**: *I
  visited the Louvre in 2019.* A common pattern in news and in IELTS answers: open with the
  present perfect, then give the details in the past simple: *I have been to Finland once. I
  went in winter and I saw the northern lights.*

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

El inglés usa las dos formas como dos tomas de cámara distintas del pasado: el present
perfect para la experiencia de vida hasta ahora (sin fecha), para una acción pasada con un
resultado que se ve ahora, y para una situación que empezó en el pasado y sigue siendo
verdad; el past simple para un evento terminado en un tiempo terminado, o para los detalles
de una historia. Un patrón común en noticias y en respuestas de IELTS: abrir con el present
perfect y luego dar los detalles en past simple.

  🇬🇧 *I've already eaten, thanks.* (British English uses the present perfect with

- 🇪🇸 *Vivo en Medellín desde 2021.* (presente + "desde")
  🇬🇧 *I have lived in Medellín since 2021.* (the present alone only covers now; a

- 🇪🇸 *¿Alguna vez fuiste a Suecia?* (pasado simple para una experiencia de vida)
  🇬🇧 *Have you ever been to Sweden?* (life experience up to now, no date: present perfect.
  *Been to* means you went and came back).

## 4. How to build it (form)

The scene for the present perfect: **who → have/has (standing in now) → past participle →
what → no finished time**. The scene for the past simple: **who → past form → what → when
(finished time)**.

| | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present perfect: I/You/We/They | I have seen it (I've seen it) | I have not seen it (I haven't seen it) | Have you seen it? |
| Present perfect: He/She/It | She has seen it (she's seen it) | She has not seen it (she hasn't seen it) | Has she seen it? |
| Past simple: all persons | I saw it yesterday | I did not see it (I didn't see it) | Did you see it? |

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

La escena del present perfect es: quién → have/has (parado en el ahora) → participio pasado
→ qué → sin tiempo terminado. La escena del past simple es: quién → forma de pasado → qué →
cuándo (tiempo terminado). En el present perfect, *have* se mueve adelante para preguntar y
lleva el *not* para negar; en el past simple, esa tarea la hace *did*, y el verbo vuelve a su
forma base (*Did you see*, nunca *Did you saw*).
</details>

### Contraste ES → EN (forma)

- 🇪🇸 *He terminado. Tengo una laptop.* (dos verbos distintos: "haber" solo arma el tiempo,
  "tener" es posesión)
  🇬🇧 *I have finished. I have a laptop.* (the same verb *have* does both jobs: it builds
  the tense and it means possession).
- 🇪🇸 *Todavía no termino el informe.* ("todavía no" va adelante, y el verbo puede ir en
  presente)
  🇬🇧 *I haven't finished the report yet.* (present perfect, not present, and *yet* goes at
  the end. *Already* goes between *have* and the participle: *I have already finished*).
- 🇪🇸 *No he estado nunca en Finlandia.* (dos negaciones juntas: "no" + "nunca")
  🇬🇧 *I have never been to Finland.* (only one negative word: *never*, or *not ... ever*:
  *I haven't ever been*. Never *"I haven't never been"*).

## 5. Why it works this way

This lesson depends on one Big Idea from `docs/ideas-clave.md`: **English marks time and
completion with small "tool verbs", not endings** (idea 4). Here is the main Why chain, plus
three shorter causal notes: the time words that go with the present perfect, how Spanish
*he comido / comí* compares, and a Portuguese trap.

### Main Why chain: why English has a form that connects the past to now

**1. The big picture.** English builds many tenses by putting a small tool verb in front of
the main verb (see Big Idea 4, "English marks time and completion with small tool verbs").
In the present perfect, the tool verb is *have*, and it is in the **present** tense. That is
the key: a present perfect sentence stands in the present and looks back, while a past
simple sentence stands in the past.

**2. Where it comes from.** The linguist Xinyue Yao (Journal of English Linguistics, 2014,
as reported by VOA Learning English) traced the modern present perfect to the Old English
structure *have* + object + past participle, which often suggested a kind of possession: an
old sentence like *I have finished my work* meant something closer to "I have my work in a
finished condition." Over time the possession meaning faded. English also had a competing
perfect with *be* + participle, which declined: in older texts you still find it, as in
Shakespeare's *"Madam, the Lady Valeria is come to visit you"* (*Coriolanus*). Today the
perfect in English is always built with *have*.

**3. The problem it creates.** Now English has two ways to report something finished:
*I finished* and *I have finished*. The listener needs to know which picture you mean: are
you pointing at the moment in the past, or at how things stand now because of it?

**4. English's solution.** English splits the job by **time frame**. If you name, or
clearly mean, a finished time (*yesterday*, *in 2019*, *last week*), you use the past
simple. If the time frame still includes now (*today*, *this week*, *ever*, *so far*,
*since 2020*), or you give no time and care about the result now, you use the present
perfect. That is why *yesterday* can't go with the present perfect: *have* puts you in the
present, *yesterday* puts you in a finished past, and a sentence can't stand in both places
at once.

**5. How native speakers see it.** *A useful mental model*: the past simple is a photo in an
album, with a date written under it. The present perfect is you, standing in today, still
holding the result in your hands (an echo of the old "possession" meaning of *have*). This
is a way to picture it, not a proven fact about how native speakers "think."

<summary>Ver en español (Why chain completa)</summary>

Esta lección depende de una idea clave de `docs/ideas-clave.md`: el inglés marca el tiempo y
la finalización con "verbos herramienta" pequeños, no con terminaciones (idea 4).

**1. El panorama general.** El inglés arma muchos tiempos verbales poniendo un verbo
herramienta pequeño delante del verbo principal. En el present perfect, el verbo herramienta
es *have*, y está en **presente**. Esa es la clave: una oración en present perfect se para en
el presente y mira hacia atrás, mientras que una oración en past simple se para en el pasado.

**2. De dónde viene.** La lingüista Xinyue Yao (Journal of English Linguistics, 2014, según
lo reporta VOA Learning English) rastreó el present perfect moderno hasta la estructura del
inglés antiguo *have* + objeto + participio pasado, que muchas veces sugería una especie de
posesión: una oración antigua como *I have finished my work* significaba algo más cercano a
"tengo mi trabajo en estado terminado". Con el tiempo, el sentido de posesión se fue
perdiendo. El inglés también tenía un perfecto rival con *be* + participio, que decayó: en
textos antiguos todavía aparece, como en *"Madam, the Lady Valeria is come to visit you"* de
Shakespeare (*Coriolanus*). Hoy el perfecto en inglés siempre se arma con *have*.

**3. El problema que genera.** Ahora el inglés tiene dos formas de contar algo terminado:
*I finished* y *I have finished*. Quien escucha necesita saber cuál imagen quieres dar:
¿estás señalando el momento en el pasado, o cómo están las cosas ahora por culpa de eso?

**4. La solución del inglés.** El inglés reparte el trabajo según el **marco de tiempo**. Si
nombras, o das a entender claramente, un tiempo terminado (*yesterday*, *in 2019*, *last
week*), usas el past simple. Si el marco de tiempo todavía incluye el ahora (*today*, *this
week*, *ever*, *so far*, *since 2020*), o no das tiempo y te importa el resultado ahora, usas
el present perfect. Por eso *yesterday* no puede ir con el present perfect: *have* te pone en
el presente, *yesterday* te pone en un pasado terminado, y una oración no puede pararse en los
dos lugares a la vez.

**5. Cómo lo ven los hablantes nativos.** *Un modelo mental útil*: el past simple es una foto
en un álbum, con la fecha escrita debajo. El present perfect eres tú, parada en el hoy,
todavía con el resultado en las manos (un eco del viejo sentido de "posesión" de *have*). Es
una forma de imaginarlo, no un hecho comprobado sobre cómo "piensan" los hablantes nativos.

### Also: why for / since, ever / never, already / yet / just go with the present perfect

Each of these words measures the stretch of time between the past and now, so they fit the
tense that stands in now and looks back. *For* gives the length of that stretch (*for three
years*: a duration) and *since* gives the point where it starts (*since 2006*: a starting
point); Cambridge's grammar uses exactly this contrast and marks *"since three years"* and
*"for 2006"* as wrong. *Ever* and *never* cover any moment up to now, which is why they are
used for life experience (*Have you ever tried...?*). *Just* means a very short time before
now. *Already* means before now, often earlier than expected (*I've already booked my
flight*). *Yet* means up to now, in questions and negatives, for something you still expect
(*I haven't finished my coffee yet*). One nuance: in American English the past simple is
often used with *already* and *yet* (*Did you eat yet?*), while British English prefers the
present perfect (*Have you eaten yet?*).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

Cada una de estas palabras mide el tramo de tiempo entre el pasado y el ahora, así que encajan
con el tiempo verbal que se para en el ahora y mira hacia atrás. *For* da el largo de ese
tramo (*for three years*: una duración) y *since* da el punto donde empieza (*since 2006*: un
punto de inicio); la gramática de Cambridge usa exactamente este contraste y marca *"since
three years"* y *"for 2006"* como errores. *Ever* y *never* cubren cualquier momento hasta

antes de lo esperado (*I've already booked my flight*). *Yet* significa hasta ahora, en

### Also: Spanish *he comido* vs *comí*, and why the English use can feel strange

Spanish has the same two forms (*he comido*, *comí*), but it divides the work differently,
and it changes a lot by region. The RAE calls the *pretérito perfecto compuesto* the verb
form with the most geographic variation in the Spanish-speaking world. In much of Spain you
can say *El taxi ha llegado hace un momento*, while in most countries of the Americas the
simple past is required for that meaning: *El taxi llegó hace un momento*. So if your Spanish
reaches for *comí* where English wants *I have eaten*, that is normal, not a mistake in your
Spanish. Colombia is not one single case: according to the Universidad de Piura's language
service, quoting the RAE's *Nueva gramática*, Andean Colombian Spanish keeps a *comí / he
comido* contrast similar to central Spain's, while places like Chile and much of Argentina
use *comí* for almost everything. Either way, English has its own rule, and it is stricter
than Spanish in one direction: a finished time (*ago*, *yesterday*, *in 2019*) always takes
the past simple. So even Spain's *ha llegado hace un momento* becomes *arrived a moment ago*
in English, never *has arrived a moment ago*.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

manera, y cambia mucho según la región. La RAE dice que el pretérito perfecto compuesto es el

llegó hace un momento*. Así que si tu español busca *comí* donde el inglés quiere *I have

centro de España, mientras que lugares como Chile y gran parte de Argentina usan *comí* para
casi todo. En cualquier caso, el inglés tiene su propia regla, y es más estricta que el
español en una dirección: un tiempo terminado (*ago*, *yesterday*, *in 2019*) siempre lleva
past simple. Por eso incluso el *ha llegado hace un momento* de España se dice *arrived a
moment ago* en inglés, nunca *has arrived a moment ago*.

</details>

### Also: a Portuguese trap, *tenho comido*

You also speak Portuguese, so watch this one. Portuguese builds a form that looks like the
English present perfect with *ter* (*eu tenho comido*), but it means a repeated action, so it
translates as *I have been eating*, not *I have eaten*. For the English *I have already
eaten*, Portuguese usually says *Eu já comi*. So don't map *tenho comido* onto *I have
eaten*.

<summary>Ver en español</summary>

## 6. When NOT to use it (traps)

- ❌ *"I have seen him yesterday."* → ✅ *"I saw him yesterday."*
  *Why:* *yesterday* is finished time; *have* puts the sentence in the present, so the two
  clash. Finished time always takes the past simple.
- ❌ *"I live here since 2020."* → ✅ *"I have lived here since 2020."*
  *Why:* it's a calque of *vivo aquí desde 2020*. The English present only covers now;
  *since* points to a starting point in the past, so you need the present perfect to build
  the bridge from 2020 to today.
- ❌ *"I am here for two years."* (meaning *llevo dos años aquí*) → ✅ *"I have been here for
  two years."*
  *Why:* the same calque with *be*. *I am here for two years* can sound like your whole stay
  will last two years, not that two years have already passed.
- ❌ *"I have worked here since three years."* / *"I have worked here for 2020."* → ✅
  *"I have worked here for three years."* / *"I have worked here since 2020."*
  *Why:* *for* + a duration (how long), *since* + a starting point (from when). Spanish
  *desde hace* mixes both ideas, so it's easy to swap them.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español (por qué de cada trampa)</summary>

1. *Yesterday* es tiempo terminado; *have* pone la oración en el presente, así que chocan.
   El tiempo terminado siempre lleva past simple.
2. Es un calco de *vivo aquí desde 2020*. El presente en inglés solo cubre el ahora; *since*
   señala un punto de inicio en el pasado, así que necesitas el present perfect para armar
   el puente desde 2020 hasta hoy.
3. El mismo calco con *be*. *I am here for two years* puede sonar a que toda tu estadía va a
   durar dos años, no a que ya pasaron dos años.
4. *For* + una duración (cuánto tiempo), *since* + un punto de inicio (desde cuándo). El
   *desde hace* del español mezcla las dos ideas, así que es fácil cambiarlas.
</details>

## 7. Hack

**Photo or bridge?** Ask one question: does the time have a date that is over? If yes, it's
a **photo**: past simple (*I saw it in 2019*). If the time still touches now, or there's no
date at all, it's a **bridge**: *have* + participle, standing in today (*I have seen it*,
*I have lived here since 2020*).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

**¿Foto o puente?** Hazte una sola pregunta: ¿el tiempo tiene una fecha que ya terminó? Si
sí, es una **foto**: past simple (*I saw it in 2019*). Si el tiempo todavía toca el ahora, o
no hay fecha, es un **puente**: *have* + participio, parada en el hoy (*I have seen it*, *I
have lived here since 2020*).
</details>

## 9. Read

*Theme: art and attention.*

I **have visited** the same small museum in Bogotá eleven times, and I **have never seen**
the same painting twice. That sounds impossible, but it's true: the paintings **haven't
changed**; I **have**. The first time I **went**, in 2019, I **walked** through every room in
forty minutes and **left** with nothing. Last year, a guide **asked** our group to stand in
front of one canvas for ten full minutes. Nobody **spoke**. After five minutes, I **noticed**
a tiny figure in the corner: a woman reading a letter.

Since that afternoon, I **have tried** to look at everything more slowly: interfaces, faces,
even my own notes. I **haven't mastered** it yet; my phone still wins most days. But I **have
learned** one thing: attention is not something you have, it's something you give.

So, **have** you ever **stood** in front of one thing for ten minutes? No? Then maybe you
**haven't** really **seen** it yet.

## 10. Summary

1. Past simple = a photo with a finished date (*yesterday*, *in 2019*, *ago*); present
   perfect = a bridge from the past to now (*ever*, *so far*, *since 2020*, or no date).
2. Build the present perfect with *have/has* + past participle; *have* is present, so it
   never goes with a finished time.
3. Most important trap: *"I live here since 2020"* is a calque; say *"I have lived here
   since 2020"* (*for* + duration, *since* + starting point).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

1. Past simple = una foto con fecha terminada (*yesterday*, *in 2019*, *ago*); present
   perfect = un puente del pasado al ahora (*ever*, *so far*, *since 2020*, o sin fecha).
2. El present perfect se arma con *have/has* + participio pasado; *have* está en presente,
   así que nunca va con un tiempo terminado.
3. La trampa más importante: *"I live here since 2020"* es un calco; di *"I have lived here
   since 2020"* (*for* + duración, *since* + punto de inicio).
</details>

Todas las URLs de abajo fueron buscadas y leídas (fetch exitoso) en esta sesión, 2026-09-26:

- [A Journey with the Present Perfect, Part One, VOA Learning English](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/a/a-journey-with-the-present-perfect-part-one-/5752780.html):
  resume el trabajo de Xinyue Yao (Journal of English Linguistics, 2014): el present perfect
  viene de la estructura *have* + participio del inglés antiguo, que muchas veces sugería
  posesión ("tengo mi trabajo en estado terminado"); el perfecto con *be* decayó.
- [Present perfect, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present_perfect): explica que la
  forma es "present" porque usa el presente de *have*; que cuando se especifica un tiempo
  pasado terminado se usa el past simple; los ejemplos de perfecto con *be* en inglés moderno
  temprano (*the Lady Valeria is come*, Shakespeare); que *haber* en español solo es auxiliar
  y la posesión va con *tener*; y que en portugués *tenho comido* significa *I have been
  eating* y *já comi* equivale a *I have already eaten*.
- [Present perfect simple, Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/present-perfect-simple-i-have-worked):
  uso de *ever/never*, *just*, *already*, *yet*, y el contraste *for* (duración) / *since*
  (punto de inicio), con *"since three years"* y *"for 2006"* marcados como errores; el
  inglés americano usa a menudo past simple con *already* y *yet*.
- [Pretérito perfecto compuesto de indicativo, Glosario de términos gramaticales, RAE y ASALE](https://www.rae.es/gtg/pret%C3%A9rito-perfecto-compuesto-de-indicativo):
  es el tiempo verbal con más variación geográfica; en buena parte de España *El taxi ha
  llegado hace un momento*, en la mayor parte de los países americanos *El taxi llegó hace un
  momento*.
- [Duda resuelta: Tiempo pasado, Castellano Actual, Universidad de Piura](https://www.udep.edu.pe/castellanoactual/duda-resuelta-tiempo-pasado/):
  citando la *Nueva gramática* (2009), ubica el contraste *comí / he comido* parecido al del
  centro de España también en el español andino colombiano, y la neutralización a favor de
