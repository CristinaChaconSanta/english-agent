## 1. Goal

Today you learn to choose between *in*, *on* and *at* (prepositions of place and time)
by picturing a shape, not by memorizing a list. Spanish covers all three with one word,
*en*, so this is where B1 speakers still slip: *"in Monday"*, *"on the morning"*,
*"arrive to"*. In lesson 01 you already put *be* next to a place (*The button is in the
corner*); these three small words decide what kind of place it is, and a wrong one
makes any description less clear.

## 2. Words you need

Technical terms you'll see in this lesson, explained before you need them:

- **Preposition**: a small word that goes before a noun and shows how that noun relates
  to the rest of the sentence in space, time or logic (*in*, *on*, *at*, *under*, *to*).
  It exists because English uses small separate words, not word endings, to show these
  relationships (see Big Idea 1).
- **Trajector**: the thing you are placing or describing, the one that gets the
  attention. In *"The keys are in the box,"* the trajector is *the keys*. It exists as a
  term so we can talk about "the thing that is somewhere" without naming it every time.
- **Landmark**: the reference thing you use to place the trajector. In *"The keys are in
  the box,"* the landmark is *the box*. It exists because you can only say where
  something is by comparing it with something else; the preposition describes the
  landmark's shape.
- **Article**: the small words *a*, *an* and *the* before a noun (Spanish *el*, *la*,
  *un*, *una*). It matters here because Spanish uses an article where English uses a
  preposition: *el lunes* becomes *on Monday*, with no *the*.
- **Fixed expression**: a group of words that native speakers always say the same way,
  even if the logic behind it is weak or lost (*at night*, *on time*). It exists because
  languages keep old habits; you learn these as whole blocks, not by rule.
- **Variety (of English)**: a version of English used in one region, such as British
  English or American English. It matters because a few of these prepositions change
  between varieties (*at the weekend* in Britain, *on the weekend* in the United States),
  and both are correct.
- **Cognitive linguistics**: a branch of linguistics that studies grammar as a way of
  picturing scenes, not as a set of empty rules. It's where the terms *trajector* and
  *landmark* come from.

## 3. What it's for (uses)

*In*, *on* and *at* do four jobs that Spanish handles almost entirely with *en*:

- **Place, where something is**: *I'll wait at the door.* / *Your notes are on the
  desk.* / *The charger is in my bag.*
- **Time, when something happens**: *The talk starts at 5 pm.* / *We present on
  Monday.* / *The course ends in July.*
- **Places seen as an activity, not a building**: *She's at work.* / *He did well at
  university.* / *Were you at the party?*
- **The end point of a trip, with *arrive***: *We arrived at the station* (a point). /
  *We arrived in Stockholm* (a city, an area).

<summary>Ver en español</summary>

*In*, *on* y *at* hacen cuatro trabajos que el español resuelve casi todos con *en*:
lugar (dónde está algo), tiempo (cuándo pasa algo), lugares vistos como una actividad y
no como un edificio (*at work*, *at university*, *at the party*), y el punto final de un
viaje con *arrive* (*arrive at* para un punto, *arrive in* para una ciudad o un país).

  🇬🇧 *The book is on the table. The keys are in the box. I'll wait for you at the

- 🇪🇸 *Nací en 1998, en julio, un lunes, a las 5 de la mañana.*

  switches to *a* for the clock)
- 🇪🇸 *Estoy en el trabajo. Estudio en la universidad. Estaba en una fiesta.*
  🇬🇧 *I'm at work. I study at university. I was at a party.* (*at* = the place where
  an activity happens; *in the university* would mean "inside the building")

## 4. How to build it (form)

The scene: **who → does what → where (point, surface or box) → when (point, surface or
box)**. The preposition goes right before the landmark, and it doesn't change in
negatives or questions:

**preposition + (the / a / my) + landmark**: *at the door*, *on Monday*, *in July*.

| Image | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **at**: a point | The talk starts at 5 pm, at the main door. | The talk doesn't start at 5 pm. | What time does it start? At 5 pm. |
| **on**: a surface or a line | Your notes are on the desk. We present on Monday. | Your notes aren't on the desk. | When do we present? On Monday. |
| **in**: a container or an area | The charger is in my bag. The course ends in July. | The charger isn't in my bag. | When does the course end? In July. |

No preposition at all before *this*, *next*, *last*, *every*: *See you next Friday.* /
*I read every morning.* Never *"on next Friday"*.

## 5. Why it works this way

This lesson touches two Big Ideas from `docs/ideas-clave.md`: **English leans on word
order and small helper words, not word endings** (idea 1, where prepositions are one of
those helper words) and **English packs *how* into the verb and *which way* into a small
word** (idea 3, the idea behind the trajector and landmark scene). Here is the main Why
chain, for the three-way split, plus three shorter notes: why the same images work for
time, why some expressions break the pattern, and why *arrive* never takes *to*.

### Main Why chain: why English needs three words where Spanish uses one

**1. The big picture.** English carries relationships with small separate words, and
prepositions are some of the busiest of them (see Big Idea 1). For location, English
goes one step further than Spanish: it makes you say what shape the landmark has. Is it
a point, a surface, or a container? Spanish *en* doesn't ask; the context does that
work.

**2. Where it comes from.** Spanish *en* comes from Latin *in*, and today one Spanish
word covers both "inside" (*Estoy en Bogotá*) and "on top of a surface" (*Dejé el
sombrero en la mesa*). English *in*, *on* and *at* are old Germanic words: Old English
already had *in*, *on* and *æt*. But the split was not tidy at the start. Old English
*in* could mean "in, into, upon, on, at, among," and *on* was used in many places where
today we'd say *in*. The clear line between *in* and *on* comes from later Middle
English. So the three-way system is something English sharpened over centuries, not a
law it always had.

**3. The problem it creates.** Once English has three words, every sentence that places
something forces a choice: which shape is this landmark? A Spanish speaker's first
language never asks that question, so the choice feels random, and a list of rules
(*"on for days, in for months, at for times…"*) is hard to remember because it hides
the reason.

**4. English's solution.** The choice follows one picture of the landmark:
- **at** = the landmark is **a point**. You don't care about its size or inside, only
  "there": *at the door*, *at my desk*, *at the bus stop*.
- **on** = the landmark is **a surface or a line** the trajector touches or runs
  along: *on the table*, *on the wall*, *on the third floor*, *on the east coast*.
- **in** = the landmark is **a container or an area** around the trajector: *in the
  box*, *in the room*, *in Stockholm*, *in Colombia*.

The key word is *see*: Cambridge's grammar says *at* is for a location "which we see as
a point." The same airport can be *at the airport* (our meeting point) or *in the
airport* (inside the building). You choose the picture; the grammar follows it.

**5. How native speakers see it.** *A useful mental model*: native speakers don't check
a list; they zoom. Zoomed out, a place becomes a pin on a map (*at*). Zoomed in to a
flat thing, it's a floor or a line you stand on (*on*). Zoomed into something with
walls or borders, it's a box you are inside (*in*). This is a way to picture the choice,
not a proven description of what happens in a native speaker's head.

### Also: why the same three images work for time

Cambridge's grammar lists the time uses like this: *at* for points on the clock (*at
five o'clock*), *on* for days and dates (*on Monday*, *on 5 October*), *in* for parts
of the day, months, years, seasons and long periods (*in the morning*, *in July*, *in
2027*). Researchers who study how people think about time report that spatial
language is used to structure time, and findings suggest that temporal uses like *at
noon*, *on Wednesday* and *in June* keep some of their spatial meaning. Other researchers argue
that these time uses are frozen idioms with no picture left, so this is still debated.

What follows is *a useful mental model*, not a finding: **at 5 pm** is a pin on the
clock, a point with no inside. **On Monday** is a day you stand on, like a floor: one
flat unit. **In July** or **in 2027** is a container of time, big enough to have things
happen inside it. The bigger the stretch of time, the more it feels like a box: minutes
are points, days are floors, months and years are rooms.

siente como una caja: los minutos son puntos, los días son pisos, los meses y los años
son cuartos.

### Also: why some expressions break the pattern

Some of this is predictable, and some is plain convention. Here is which is which:

- ***In the morning*, but *on Monday morning*.** Predictable. Cambridge's grammar says
  *in* goes with *morning*, *afternoon*, *evening* and *night* in general, but *on* goes
  with a specific morning (*on the morning of 9 November*). Once you attach the part of
  the day to one day, the day's picture (*on*) wins.
- ***At night*, but *in the morning*.** Mostly convention. *At night* is a fixed
  expression for "any night, at night in general," while *in the night* exists too and
  usually means during one particular night (*I woke up in the night*). The grammar
  describes this difference but gives no deeper reason, and the Oxford English
  Dictionary, quoted by Etymonline, admits that *at* has so many uses that classifying
  them is very difficult. Learn *at night* as a block.
- ***At the weekend* (British) and *on the weekend* (American).** Pure variety
  difference, and both are correct. British English groups *weekend* with public
  holidays, which also take *at* (*at the New Year*). American English says *on the
  weekend*; you can picture it treating the weekend like days (*on Saturday*). *In the
  weekend* is wrong in both.
- ***On the bus*, but *in the car*.** Partly a pattern, partly convention. Cambridge
  uses *on* for being physically on public transport (*on the train*). The linguist
  Geoffrey Pullum lists pairs like *in a car / on a bus / in a taxi / on a ship* and
  says the choice is not fully predictable from meaning or vehicle shape. His hunch is
  that three things may matter: your normal position in the vehicle, its size compared
  with a person, and whether it runs on a schedule. A rule of thumb (not a rule): if you can
  stand up and walk around inside it, it's usually *on*; if you just sit in a small
  closed space, it's *in*.

### Also: why *arrive* takes *at* or *in*, never *to*

Spanish thinks of arriving as a movement toward a goal: *llegar **a***, with the same
*a* you use for direction (*voy a Bogotá*). English *arrive* describes the moment you
end up located somewhere, so it takes a location word: *at* when you see the place as a
point (*arrive at the station*, *arrive at the hotel*), *in* for a bigger area like a
city or a country (*arrive in Italy*, *arrive in Tokyo*). Cambridge's grammar says it
directly: "We don't use *to* with *arrive*." With *home*, *here* and *there*, use no
preposition at all: *arrive home*. The English rule is backed by Cambridge's grammar;
the "movement versus ending up somewhere" picture is *a useful mental model* to
remember it.

## 6. When NOT to use it (traps)

- ❌ *"The demo is in Monday."* → ✅ *"The demo is on Monday."*
  *Why:* days and dates take *on* (a day is a surface); *in* is for longer containers of
  time like months and years.
- ❌ *"I write better on the morning."* → ✅ *"I write better in the morning."*
  *Why:* parts of the day in general take *in*; use *on* only when you attach them to one
  specific day (*on Monday morning*).
- ❌ *"What are you doing in the weekend?"* → ✅ *"What are you doing at the weekend?"*
  (British) / *"What are you doing on the weekend?"* (American)
  *Why:* *in the weekend* is wrong in both varieties; pick one variety and stay
  consistent in the IELTS exam.
- ❌ *"We arrived to Stockholm at midnight."* → ✅ *"We arrived in Stockholm at
  midnight."*
  *Why:* *arrive* never takes *to*; use *in* for a city or country and *at* for a point
  like a station or a building.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español (por qué de cada trampa)</summary>

1. Los días y las fechas llevan *on* (un día es una superficie); *in* es para
   recipientes de tiempo más largos, como meses y años.
2. Las partes del día en general llevan *in*; usa *on* solo cuando las pegas a un día
   concreto (*on Monday morning*).
3. *In the weekend* está mal en las dos variedades; elige una variedad y mantenla en el
   examen IELTS.
4. *Arrive* nunca lleva *to*; usa *in* para una ciudad o un país y *at* para un punto
   como una estación o un edificio (ver sección 5).
</details>

## 7. Hack

**Pin, floor, box.** Before you pick a preposition, ask what the landmark looks like
from where you stand. A pin on a map or a clock is *at* (*at the door*, *at 5 pm*). A
floor you stand on is *on* (*on the table*, *on Monday*). A box you are inside is *in*
(*in Bogotá*, *in July*). Who → does what → where (pin, floor or box) → when (pin, floor
or box).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

**Alfiler, piso, caja.** Antes de elegir una preposición, pregúntate cómo se ve el
landmark desde donde estás. Un alfiler en un mapa o en un reloj es *at* (*at the door*,
*at 5 pm*). Un piso sobre el que estás parado es *on* (*on the table*, *on Monday*). Una
caja dentro de la cual estás es *in* (*in Bogotá*, *in July*). Quién → hace qué → dónde
(alfiler, piso o caja) → cuándo (alfiler, piso o caja).
</details>

## 9. Read

*Theme: HCI (human-computer interaction) and everyday design.*

**At** 7:15 **on** a grey Tuesday **in** November, Lucía stood **at** the bus stop **on**
Carrera Séptima with a notebook **in** her hand. She was not waiting for the bus. She was
studying the people waiting for it.

Her question was simple: what do people do with ninety seconds of nothing? Some checked
their phones. A man **in** a red coat read the timetable **on** the glass three times.
Two students **on** the bench argued about a film they had seen **at** the weekend.

**In** her report, Lucía wrote that a bus stop is not really a place; it is a pause.
Nobody lives **at** a bus stop. People only pass through it, usually **in** the morning
and again **at** night. That is why, she argued, screens **at** bus stops should not
shout. They should answer one question: how long?

She got **on** the 7:32, found a seat **in** the back, and kept writing. People **in**
cars, she noted later, never get these ninety seconds. They lose them **in** traffic
instead.

*(171 words)*

<details>
<summary>Ver en español (traducción, para revisar comprensión después de leer)</summary>

A las 7:15 de un martes gris de noviembre, Lucía estaba parada en la parada de bus de la
Carrera Séptima con una libreta en la mano. No estaba esperando el bus. Estaba
estudiando a la gente que lo esperaba.

Su pregunta era simple: ¿qué hace la gente con noventa segundos de nada? Algunos
revisaban el celular. Un hombre de abrigo rojo leyó el horario pegado en el vidrio tres
veces. Dos estudiantes en la banca discutían sobre una película que habían visto el fin
de semana.

En su informe, Lucía escribió que una parada de bus no es realmente un lugar; es una
pausa. Nadie vive en una parada de bus. La gente solo pasa por ahí, casi siempre en la
mañana y otra vez en la noche. Por eso, argumentaba, las pantallas de las paradas no
deberían gritar. Deberían responder una sola pregunta: ¿cuánto falta?

Se subió al de las 7:32, encontró un puesto atrás y siguió escribiendo. La gente que va
en carro, anotó después, nunca tiene esos noventa segundos. Los pierde en el trancón.
</details>

## 10. Summary

1. *At* is a point, *on* is a surface or a line, *in* is a container or an area; the
   same three pictures work for place (*at the door*, *on the desk*, *in the box*) and
   time (*at 5 pm*, *on Monday*, *in July*).
2. Spanish *el lunes* becomes *on Monday*, and *next*, *last*, *this*, *every* take no
   preposition at all.
3. The big traps: *on* Monday (not *in*), *in* the morning (not *on*), *at/on the
   weekend* (never *in*), and *arrive in/at* (never *arrive to*).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

1. *At* es un punto, *on* es una superficie o una línea, *in* es un recipiente o un
   área; las mismas tres imágenes sirven para lugar (*at the door*, *on the desk*, *in
   the box*) y tiempo (*at 5 pm*, *on Monday*, *in July*).
2. *El lunes* del español se vuelve *on Monday*, y *next*, *last*, *this*, *every* no
   llevan ninguna preposición.
3. Las trampas grandes: *on* Monday (no *in*), *in* the morning (no *on*), *at/on the
   weekend* (nunca *in*) y *arrive in/at* (nunca *arrive to*).
</details>

- [At, on and in (time), Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/at-on-and-in-time):
  *at* para puntos del reloj, *on* para días y fechas, *in* para partes del día, meses,
  años y periodos largos; *on* para una mañana específica; *at night* frente a *in the
  night*; *at* con festivos y fines de semana; *in the weekend* marcado como error; nota
  de que en inglés americano se dice *on the weekend*; sin preposición antes de *next*,
  *every*, *this*, *last*.
- [At, on and in (place), Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/at-on-and-in-place):
  *at* para un lugar "que vemos como un punto", para actividades y escuela/universidad;
  *on* para superficies, calles, costas, pisos de un edificio y transporte público;
  *in* para lugares dentro de un área más grande.
- [At, in and to (movement), Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/at-in-and-to-movement):
  *arrive at* para un punto, *arrive in* para un área grande, y "We don't use *to* with

- [British and American English, Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/british-and-american-english):
  "BrE prefers *at the weekend*; AmE prefers *on the weekend*".
- [Weekend, Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/weekend):
  acento británico en la segunda sílaba y americano en la primera; *at the weekend* UK

- [In the car, on the bus, Language Log (Geoffrey K. Pullum)](http://itre.cis.upenn.edu/~myl/languagelog/archives/005224.html):
  la elección *in/on* con vehículos no es del todo predecible por significado ni por la
  forma del vehículo; posibles factores: posición normal, tamaño y horario fijo.
- [In, Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/in): el *in* del inglés antiguo
  significaba "in, into, upon, on, at, among"; la distinción entre *in* y *on* viene del
  inglés medio tardío.
- [On, Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/on): *on* del inglés antiguo, también
  usado donde hoy se diría *in*.
- [At, Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/at): *at* del inglés antiguo *æt*;
  cita del OED sobre lo difícil que es clasificar los usos de *at*; *at church* (con un
  propósito práctico) frente a *in the church*.
- [en, Wikcionario](https://es.wiktionary.org/wiki/en): *en* viene del latín *in*;

- [The Functional Organization of English Clauses, Ronald W. Langacker](https://www.usc-vlcg.es/LANGACKER.pdf):
  definición de *trajector* (el participante principal, lo que se ubica) y *landmark*

- [Prepositions retain aspects of spatial meaning in abstract contexts, Jamrozik y Gentner (2011)](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/jamrozikGentner_2011.pdf):
  el lenguaje espacial se usa para estructurar el tiempo, y usos como *at noon*, *on
  Wednesday*, *in June* conservan contenido semántico espacial; también menciona la
  postura contraria (usos abstractos como significados idiomáticos congelados).
