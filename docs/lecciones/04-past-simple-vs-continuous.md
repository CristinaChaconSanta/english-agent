# Lesson 04: Past simple vs past continuous for storytelling

*Block 1, unit 1. Level: B1 (Core Inventory, "Verb forms" #67 and #68). IELTS: Fluency and
Coherence, Grammatical Range and Accuracy.*

## 1. Goal

Today you learn to tell a story in the past with two forms that work as a team: the
**past simple** (*I called*) for the events, and the **past continuous** (*I was
calling*) for the action that was already running around them. This is unit 1 of Block
1 in the syllabus because every story you tell in IELTS Speaking Part 2, and every
personal example in Writing Task 2, depends on it. It also fixes the most common Spanish
speaker mistake here: using *"I was going"* for a habit, because the Spanish *imperfecto*
covers habits and English does not work that way.

**Success measure:** 5 new Check items, pass with 4/5.

## 2. Words you need

Technical terms you'll see in this lesson, explained before you need them:

- **Tense**: the form of the verb that places an action in time (past or present). It
  exists so the listener knows *when* something happened: *I call* (now, in general) vs.
  *I called* (before now).
- **Aspect**: the form of the verb that shows *how* you look at an action in time:
  as a finished whole, or from the inside while it is still happening. It exists
  because "when" is not enough: two actions can both be in the past and still look very
  different (one finished, one still running).
- **Past simple**: the past form of the main verb alone (*called*, *went*, *knew*). It
  exists to show a past action as one complete block, with a start and an end: a
  photo.
- **Past continuous (also called past progressive)**: *was/were* + verb with *-ing*
  (*was calling*, *were cooking*). It exists to show a past action from the inside,
  while it was in progress, without saying when it started or ended: a film running.
- **-ing form**: the verb with the ending *-ing* (*calling*, *cooking*). After *be*, it
  means "in the middle of doing it". It exists so the main verb can say "in progress"
  while *be* carries the time.
- **Tool verb (auxiliary verb)**: a small helper verb (*be*, *have*, *do*, *will*) that
  carries time, questions or negatives so the main verb doesn't have to. In this lesson
  the tool verb is *was/were*, and in past simple questions and negatives it is *did*.
- **Event**: an action you see as one complete point in the story: *she called*, *the
  lights went out*. Events push the story forward.
- **Background**: the scene that was already going on when an event happened: *it was
  raining*, *I was cooking*. It exists so the listener can picture the moment before
  something changes.
- **Habit**: something you did many times, regularly: *every Sunday*, *as a child*. It
  exists as its own idea because a habit is repeated, not "in progress at one moment".
- **State verb (stative verb)**: a verb that describes a situation that stays the same
  the whole time, not an action that moves forward: *know*, *want*, *like*, *believe*,
  *own*. Nothing "happens" inside a state; you are just in it. That is why these verbs
  almost never take *-ing* (see section 5).
- **Action verb (dynamic verb)**: a verb that describes something that happens and
  changes over time: *run*, *cook*, *read*, *call*. These are the verbs that can take
  *-ing*.
- **Time clause**: a short part of the sentence that starts with *when* or *while* and
  gives the time frame for the rest: *when she called*, *while I was cooking*. It exists
  to connect two actions in time inside one sentence.

## 3. What it's for (uses)

In a story, the two forms split the work: the past simple moves the plot, the past
continuous paints the scene.

- **Events in order (past simple)**: *She read the email and called her sister.*
- **The background scene (past continuous)**: *It was raining, and people were running
  to the metro.*
- **An action in progress interrupted by an event (both)**: *I was reading when the
  power went out.*
- **Two actions in progress at the same time (past continuous twice)**: *While I was
  cooking, my flatmate was playing the piano.*

One more job the past simple keeps for itself: **past habits and past states** (*As a
child, I went to school by bus.* / *I knew the answer.*). The past continuous does not
do these.

- **Eventos en orden (past simple)**: *She read the email and called her sister.*
- **La escena de fondo (past continuous)**: *It was raining, and people were running to
  the metro.*
- **Una acción en curso interrumpida por un evento (las dos)**: *I was reading when the
  power went out.*
- **Dos acciones en curso al mismo tiempo (past continuous dos veces)**: *While I was
  cooking, my flatmate was playing the piano.*

Un trabajo más que el past simple se queda para sí: **hábitos pasados y estados
pasados** (*As a child, I went to school by bus.* / *I knew the answer.*). El past
continuous no hace esto.

### Contrast ES → EN (use)

- 🇪🇸 *Llovía y la gente corría al metro.* (imperfecto para la escena)
  🇬🇧 *It was raining and people were running to the metro.* (Here the two languages
  match: the Spanish *imperfecto* for a scene becomes the past continuous.)
- 🇪🇸 *De niña, iba al colegio en bus.* (imperfecto para un hábito)
  🇬🇧 *As a child, I went to school by bus.* / *As a child, I used to go to school by
  bus.* (Here they don't match: a habit is past simple or *used to*, never *"I was
  going"*.)
- 🇪🇸 *Sabía la respuesta.* (imperfecto para un estado)
  🇬🇧 *I knew the answer.* (*Know* is a state, so English uses the past simple, never
  *"I was knowing"*.)

## 4. How to build it (form)

Two scenes, two formulas:

- **The photo (past simple):** who → did what (past form) → to what → where → when.
  *She → called → her sister → from the office → at six.*
- **The film (past continuous):** who → was/were + verb-ing → what → where → when.
  *I → was cooking → pasta → in the kitchen → at six.*

The tool verb *was/were* carries the time (past) and the person. The main verb only adds
*-ing* and says "in progress". In the past simple there is no tool verb in affirmative
sentences, but questions and negatives need *did*, and then the main verb goes back to
its base form (*did call*, not *did called*).

| | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Past simple: I/He/She/It | I called | I didn't call | Did I call? |
| Past simple: You/We/They | They called | They didn't call | Did they call? |
| Past continuous: I/He/She/It | She was calling | She wasn't calling | Was she calling? |
| Past continuous: You/We/They | We were calling | We weren't calling | Were we calling? |

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

Dos escenas, dos fórmulas:

- **La foto (past simple):** quién → hizo qué (forma pasada) → a qué → dónde → cuándo.
  *She → called → her sister → from the office → at six.*
- **La película (past continuous):** quién → was/were + verbo-ing → qué → dónde →
  cuándo. *I → was cooking → pasta → in the kitchen → at six.*

El verbo herramienta *was/were* carga el tiempo (pasado) y la persona. El verbo
principal solo agrega *-ing* y dice "en curso". En el past simple no hay verbo
herramienta en las afirmaciones, pero las preguntas y negaciones necesitan *did*, y
entonces el verbo principal vuelve a su forma base (*did call*, no *did called*).
</details>

### Contrast ES → EN (form)

- 🇪🇸 *Leía.* / *Estaba leyendo.* (el español puede decirlo en una sola palabra, o con
  estar + gerundio)
  🇬🇧 *I was reading.* (English has no one-word version: it always needs the tool verb
  *was/were* + the *-ing* form.)
- 🇪🇸 *¿Qué hacías?* (la pregunta vive en la terminación y en el tono)
  🇬🇧 *What were you doing?* (The tool verb *were* moves in front of the subject: the
  word order is the question.)
- 🇪🇸 *No llamé.* / *No estaba llamando.* (el "no" va delante del verbo y ya)
  🇬🇧 *I didn't call.* / *I wasn't calling.* (The negative attaches to a tool verb:
  *did* in the past simple, *was* in the past continuous. Never *"I didn't called"*.)

## 5. Why it works this way

This lesson connects to **Big Idea 4, "English marks time and completion with small
tool verbs, not endings"** (`docs/ideas-clave.md`). The main Why chain answers the big
question: why English needs two past forms at all. Three shorter notes after it answer
the other three questions: where the match with Spanish fails, why some verbs refuse
*-ing*, and why *when* and *while* usually go with different forms.

### Main Why chain: why English needs two past forms

**1. The big picture.** English does not change the ending of the main verb for every
shade of meaning; it plugs in a small tool verb instead (Big Idea 4). To say "in
progress", English uses the tool verb *be* + the *-ing* form: *was cooking*. Spanish can
put the same idea inside the verb ending itself (*cocinaba*), and it also has *estaba
cocinando*.

**2. Where it comes from.** The English past continuous grew slowly. It likely came
from two constructions that were rare in Old and early Middle English: *be* + a
participle ending in *-ende*, and *be* + a preposition + a noun-like verb ending in
*-unge*. Sound changes during Middle English merged those two endings into one: *-ing*.
After that merger (complete in southern England around the late fifteenth century) the
form was used much more often, but it did not take its current shape until the
eighteenth century. One historical study found that during Middle English, *be* +
*-ing* appeared more and more inside clauses that set the background of a story, and
that this is how it picked up its meaning of "ongoing".

**3. The problem it creates.** The past simple on its own is very wide: it covers a
single event (*she called*), a habit (*I visited them every day for a year*), and a
state (*I knew*). From the verb *called* alone, a listener can't tell if you are showing
a finished event or a scene that was still running when something else happened. A
story needs to separate those two layers, or everything sounds like a list of flat,
equal events.

**4. English's solution.** Keep the past simple for the events, and add the tool verb
*was/were* + *-ing* for the action in progress. The *-ing* form puts the listener
*inside* the action, without saying how long it lasted or if it finished. In *"Andrew
was playing tennis when Jane called him"*, you learn what Andrew was in the middle of,
not how long he played. The past simple *called* is the moment that cuts into it.

**5. How native speakers see it.** *A useful mental model* (a way to picture it, not a
proven fact about how speakers think): a story is a film. The past continuous is the
footage already running in the background; the past simple is the photo flash that
freezes one moment on top of it and moves the plot forward. The film has no clear start
or end on screen; the photo is one complete click.

**2. De dónde viene.** El past continuous del inglés creció despacio. Probablemente

temprano: *be* + un participio terminado en *-ende*, y *be* + una preposición + una

mucho más, pero no tomó su forma actual hasta el siglo XVIII. Un estudio histórico

oraciones que ponían el fondo de una historia, y que así fue como adquirió su
significado de "en curso".

**3. El problema que genera.** El past simple por sí solo es muy amplio: cubre un evento
único (*she called*), un hábito (*I visited them every day for a year*) y un estado (*I
knew*). Solo con el verbo *called*, el oyente no sabe si muestras un evento terminado o
una escena que seguía en marcha cuando pasó otra cosa. Una historia necesita separar
esas dos capas, o todo suena como una lista de eventos planos e iguales.

**4. La solución del inglés.** Dejar el past simple para los eventos y agregar el verbo
herramienta *was/were* + *-ing* para la acción en curso. La forma *-ing* pone al oyente
*adentro* de la acción, sin decir cuánto duró ni si terminó. En *"Andrew was playing
tennis when Jane called him"*, sabes en medio de qué estaba Andrew, no cuánto tiempo
jugó. El past simple *called* es el momento que la corta.

**5. Cómo lo ven los hablantes nativos.** *Un modelo mental útil* (una forma de
imaginarlo, no un hecho comprobado sobre cómo piensan los hablantes): una historia es
una película. El past continuous es la grabación que ya corre de fondo; el past simple
es el flash de la foto que congela un momento encima y hace avanzar la trama. La
película no muestra un inicio ni un final claros; la foto es un solo clic completo.

### Also: where Spanish *pretérito* and *imperfecto* match English, and where they fail

Spanish splits the past into two forms too, and the split is about the same thing as in
English: aspect. The *pretérito* (*llegué*, *corrí*) shows an action as completed, like
the English past simple. The *imperfecto* (*llegaba*, *corría*) presents the action as
context, without its start or end, which is why it so often matches the English past
continuous in a story: *"Llegaba a casa cuando empezó a llover"* → *"I was getting home
when it started to rain."*

The match fails in one big place: **habits**. The Spanish *imperfecto* also covers
repeated or habitual past actions: *"corría cinco millas cada mañana"*, *"cuando era
pequeño, hablaba español con mi abuela"*. English does **not** use the past continuous
for that. It uses the past simple (*"I ran five miles every morning"*, *"When I was
young, I spoke Spanish with my grandmother"*) or *used to* (*"I used to run five miles
every morning"*). So the Spanish *imperfecto* splits into two English forms: in progress
at one moment → *was/were* + *-ing*; habit → past simple or *used to*. Before you say
*"I was ___ing"*, ask: was it one scene at one moment, or something I did many times? If
it was many times, drop the *-ing*.

<summary>Ver en español</summary>

coincide con el past continuous: *"Llegaba a casa cuando empezó a llover"* → *"I was

### Also: why some verbs almost never take *-ing* (state verbs)

A **state verb** describes a situation that is the same from beginning to end: *know*,
*want*, *like*, *believe*, *own*. In plain words: nothing moves inside it. You don't
"do" knowing step by step; you just know. The *-ing* form means "in the middle of a
process that is moving forward", and a state has no middle and no moving parts, so the
two don't fit. That is why *"I am knowing the answer"* sounds wrong to native speakers,
and why the past of a state stays in the past simple even when it is background: *"At
three o'clock yesterday we were in the garden"*, *"I knew the answer"*, *"She wanted to
leave"*.

Two details that help:

- **One verb, two meanings.** Some verbs are a state in one meaning and an action in
  another. *Think* as an opinion is a state (*I thought it was fair*); *think* as a
  process in your head is an action (*I was thinking about my essay*). *Have* as
  possession is a state (*I had a bike*); *have* as an activity is an action (*We were
  having dinner*).
- **The moment you enter a state.** The past simple of a state verb can mean the moment
  the state began: *"Suddenly he understood what she said."* Spanish does something
  similar with the *pretérito*: *supe* often means "I found out". English usually picks
  a different verb for that moment: *knew* (*sabía*) vs. *found out* (*supe*).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

*want*, *like*, *believe*, *own*. En palabras simples: adentro no se mueve nada. No
"haces" el saber paso a paso; simplemente sabes. La forma *-ing* significa "en medio de
un proceso que avanza", y un estado no tiene un medio ni partes que se muevan, así que
no encajan. Por eso *"I am knowing the answer"* suena mal a los hablantes nativos, y por

- **Un verbo, dos significados.** Algunos verbos son estado en un sentido y acción en
  otro. *Think* como opinión es estado (*I thought it was fair*); *think* como proceso
  en tu cabeza es acción (*I was thinking about my essay*). *Have* como posesión es

### Also: why *when* and *while* usually go with different forms

Look at the two classic patterns:

- *I was cooking **when** she called.*
- ***While** I was cooking, she called.*

*While* introduces a stretch of time, so what follows it is usually the long action in
progress: *while I was cooking*. *When* usually introduces the short event that cuts
in: *when she called*. Both sentences say the same thing; they just start from a
different side of the scene. *While* can also join two actions in progress at the same
time: *"While I was cooking, my flatmate was playing the piano."*

The history note in the main chain explains why this fits so well: *be* + *-ing* took
its "ongoing" meaning exactly inside clauses that set the background of a story. *A
useful mental model* for daily use: *while* opens a window of time (English still says
*for a while*, "durante un rato"), and a window needs something running inside it, so
it takes the *-ing*. *When* points at a moment, so it usually takes the one-click past
simple. "Usually" matters: *when* can also start a background (*"When I was living in
Medellín, I met her"*), but *while* + a single short event (*"While she called"*) sounds
wrong, because a phone call starting is a moment, not a stretch.

- ***While** I was cooking, she called.*

*While* introduce un tramo de tiempo, así que lo que le sigue suele ser la acción larga
en curso: *while I was cooking*. *When* suele introducir el evento corto que interrumpe:
*when she called*. Las dos oraciones dicen lo mismo; solo empiezan por un lado distinto
de la escena. *While* también puede unir dos acciones en curso al mismo tiempo: *"While
I was cooking, my flatmate was playing the piano."*

La nota histórica de la cadena principal explica por qué encaja tan bien: *be* + *-ing*
tomó su significado de "en curso" justamente dentro de oraciones que ponían el fondo de
una historia. *Un modelo mental útil* para el día a día: *while* abre una ventana de
tiempo (el inglés todavía dice *for a while*, "durante un rato"), y una ventana necesita
algo corriendo adentro, así que lleva *-ing*. *When* señala un momento, así que suele
llevar el past simple de un solo clic. El "suele" importa: *when* también puede abrir un
fondo (*"When I was living in Medellín, I met her"*), pero *while* + un solo evento corto
(*"While she called"*) suena mal, porque el inicio de una llamada es un momento, no un
tramo.

</details>

## 6. When NOT to use it (traps)

- ❌ *"I was knowing the answer, but I didn't say it."* → ✅ *"I knew the answer, but I
  didn't say it."*
  *Why:* *know* is a state verb; nothing is in progress, so it stays in the past simple.
- ❌ *"When I was a child, I was going to school by bus."* → ✅ *"When I was a child, I
  went to school by bus."* (or *"I used to go"*)
  *Why:* this is a habit. The Spanish *imperfecto* (*iba*) covers habits; the English
  past continuous does not.
- ❌ *"While she called, I cooked."* → ✅ *"While I was cooking, she called."*
  *Why:* *while* needs a stretch of time running in the background. Here nothing is in
  progress: both actions are photos, so there is no scene for the call to cut into.
- ❌ *"Yesterday I was sending my application to Aalto."* (when you mean it's done) → ✅
  *"Yesterday I sent my application to Aalto."*
  *Why:* a finished, short event is a photo. The past continuous makes it sound
  unfinished, or like the background for something else you haven't told yet.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español (por qué de cada trampa)</summary>

1. *Know* es un verbo de estado; no hay nada en curso, así que se queda en past simple.
2. Es un hábito. El *imperfecto* (*iba*) cubre hábitos; el past continuous del inglés
   no.
3. *While* necesita un tramo de tiempo corriendo de fondo. Aquí nada está en curso: las
   dos acciones son fotos, así que no hay escena que la llamada pueda interrumpir.
4. Un evento corto y terminado es una foto. El past continuous lo hace sonar sin
   terminar, o como el fondo de otra cosa que todavía no contaste.
</details>

## 7. Hack

**Film first, then the photo.** Before you tell a past moment, ask one question: *could
I pause the film in the middle of it?* If yes (it was running at that moment), use
*was/were* + *-ing*. If it was one click, a habit, or a state (*know, want, like,
believe*), use the past simple. The scene always reads: who → was doing what (the film)
→ when → who → did what (the photo).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

**Primero la película, después la foto.** Antes de contar un momento pasado, hazte una
pregunta: *¿podría pausar la película en medio de esto?* Si sí (estaba corriendo en ese
momento), usa *was/were* + *-ing*. Si fue un solo clic, un hábito o un estado (*know,
want, like, believe*), usa el past simple. La escena siempre se lee: quién → estaba
haciendo qué (la película) → cuando → quién → hizo qué (la foto).
</details>

stand up, he **looked** at the painting too. Then the lights **went** out.

In the dark, something strange **happened**. The grey **wasn't** grey anymore. Thin lines
of blue and gold **were moving** under the surface, like water under ice. Suddenly he
**understood** why she **was sitting** there.

When the lights **came** back, she **was** gone. The guard **stayed** a little longer. He
**wanted** to see it one more time.

## 10. Summary

1. The past simple is the photo (a finished event, a habit, or a state); the past
   continuous (*was/were* + *-ing*) is the film running in the background.
2. *While* usually opens the film (*while I was cooking*); *when* usually brings the
   photo that cuts in (*when she called*).
3. The biggest trap: the Spanish *imperfecto* also covers habits, but English never uses
   the past continuous for habits; use the past simple or *used to*. And state verbs
   (*know, want, like, believe*) stay in the past simple.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

1. El past simple es la foto (un evento terminado, un hábito o un estado); el past
   continuous (*was/were* + *-ing*) es la película que corre de fondo.
2. *While* suele abrir la película (*while I was cooking*); *when* suele traer la foto
   que interrumpe (*when she called*).
3. La trampa más grande: el *imperfecto* también cubre hábitos, pero el inglés nunca usa
   el past continuous para hábitos; usa el past simple o *used to*. Y los verbos de
   estado (*know, want, like, believe*) se quedan en past simple.
</details>
