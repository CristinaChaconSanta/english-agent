uses for an activity that started in the past and is still going on now, or that has
just stopped and left traces. You will also learn when English refuses it: with state
verbs like *know*, and when you count finished results.

**Why now:** lesson 05 gave you *have* (the past touching now) and lesson 04 gave you
*be + -ing* (an action in progress). This lesson stacks the two. It also fixes a very
common calque from Spanish: *"I am working here since three years."*

## 2. Words you need

Technical terms you'll see in this lesson, explained before you need them:

- **Tool verb (auxiliary verb)**: a small helper verb (*have*, *be*, *do*, *will*) that
  carries time or aspect instead of the main verb. It exists because English marks most
  of its tenses by adding these helpers, not by changing the main verb's ending (Big
  Idea 4).
- **Main verb**: the verb that carries the real action (*paint*, *wait*, *live*). It
  exists to say what happens; in this tense it always comes last, in its *-ing* form.
- **Past participle**: the third form of a verb (*worked*, *written*, *been*). It exists
  because *have* always needs this form right after it: *have worked*, *have been*.
- **-ing form**: the verb plus *-ing* (*painting*, *waiting*). It exists to show an
  action from the inside, while it is moving, not as a finished block.
- **Perfect**: a verb form built with *have* + past participle. It exists to connect a
  past event or activity to the present moment (lesson 05).
- **Continuous (also called progressive)**: a verb form built with *be* + *-ing*. It
  exists to show an action in progress at a given time (lesson 04).
- **State verb**: a verb for a situation that doesn't move or change while it lasts
  (*know*, *like*, *believe*, *own*). It exists as a separate group because English
  treats states differently from actions: states normally refuse the *-ing* form.
- **Duration**: how long something has lasted. English marks it with *for* + a length
  of time (*for three years*) or *since* + a starting point (*since 2023*).

## 3. What it's for (uses)

The present perfect continuous looks at an activity that runs up to now:

- **An activity that started in the past and is still going on**, often with *for*,
  *since* or *how long*: *I've been learning Swedish since March.*
- **An activity that has just stopped and left evidence now**: *Your eyes are red. Have
  you been crying?* / *The street is wet. It's been raining.*
- **A temporary or repeated activity in recent weeks**: *I've been sleeping badly
  lately.* / *She's been going to the museum every Sunday.*
- **Focus on the activity, not the result**: *I've been painting the room* (the work,
  maybe unfinished) vs. *I've painted the room* (done: look at it).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

El present perfect continuous mira una actividad que llega hasta ahora: una actividad

actividad que acaba de parar y dejó evidencia ahora; una actividad temporal o repetida
en las últimas semanas; y el foco en la actividad, no en el resultado (*I've been
painting the room*: el trabajo, quizás sin terminar; *I've painted the room*: listo,

- 🇪🇸 *Hace dos horas que te espero.* (presente: *espero*)

- 🇪🇸 *Tienes los ojos rojos. ¿Estuviste llorando?* (el español puede usar un pasado
  simple aquí)
  🇬🇧 *Your eyes are red. Have you been crying?* (the evidence is here now, so English
  links the activity to now with *have been*.)

## 4. How to build it (form)

The scene: **who → have (connected to now) → been (in the middle of) → -ing (the
action moving) → for/since (how long)**. Three verbs in a row, and each one does one
job.

| | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I/You/We/They | I have been working (I've been working) | I have not been working (I haven't been working) | Have you been working? |
| He/She/It | She has been working (she's been working) | She has not been working (she hasn't been working) | Has she been working? |

*How long* questions put the question words first and then *have* before the subject:
*How long have you been living here?*

<summary>Ver en español</summary>

La escena: quién → have (conectado al ahora) → been (en medio de) → -ing (la acción en
movimiento) → for/since (cuánto tiempo). Tres verbos seguidos, y cada uno hace un solo
trabajo. En las preguntas, *have* pasa antes del sujeto; con *how long*, las palabras de
pregunta van primero: *How long have you been living here?*

### Contraste ES → EN (forma)

- 🇪🇸 *Llevo tres años estudiando.* (llevar + tiempo + gerundio: el tiempo va en el
  medio)
  🇬🇧 *I have been studying for three years.* (the three verbs stay together; the time
  goes at the end, after *for*.)
- 🇪🇸 *Estudio inglés desde hace dos años.* (presente + *desde hace* + cantidad)
  🇬🇧 *I have been studying English for two years.* (*for* + length of time; *since*
  only takes a starting point: *since 2024*, never *"since two years"*.)
- 🇪🇸 *¿Cuánto tiempo llevas viviendo aquí?*
  🇬🇧 *How long have you been living here?* (no *llevar*: *have* moves in front of the
  subject to make the question.)

## 5. Why it works this way

This lesson depends on **Big Idea 4: English marks time and completion with small "tool
verbs"** (`docs/ideas-clave.md`). It also joins two lessons you already know: lesson 04
(*was/were + -ing*, the action in progress) and lesson 05 (*have* + past participle,
the past connected to now).

### Main Why chain: why English stacks two tool verbs

**1. The big picture.** English doesn't change the main verb's ending to show time or
completion; it plugs in tool verbs, and each tool verb does one job (Big Idea 4).
*Have* says "this reaches now." *Be + -ing* says "this is in progress." When English
needs both meanings at once, it doesn't invent a new ending: it uses both tools, one
after the other.

**2. Where it comes from.** The *be + -ing* part most likely grew out of two rare Old
and Early Middle English patterns: *be* + a present participle ending in *-ende*, and
*be* + a preposition + a noun ending in *-unge*. During Middle English, sound changes
merged *-ende* and *-unge* into one ending, *-ing*; after that, the progressive became
much more common, and it reached its current form around the eighteenth century. A
peer-reviewed paper on its history adds that in Old English, *be + -ing* mostly
described a temporary state, working like an adjective; the "in progress" meaning grew
stronger later. This session found no verified source for when exactly the stacked form
*have been + -ing* first appeared, so no date is given for it here.

**3. The problem it creates.** Now English has two separate tools, and neither one is
enough alone. *Have* + past participle connects to now, but it often sounds finished:
*I've written the essay* means it's done. *Be + -ing* shows something in progress, but
only at one moment: *I am sitting here for three hours* doesn't describe the three hours
behind you; it sounds like a plan for the next three hours. So how do you say "this
activity started back then, has been moving the whole time, and reaches right now"?

**4. English's solution.** Stack the two tools: **have + been + -ing**. *Have* comes
first and links to now. Because *have* always takes a past participle after it, *be*
turns into *been*. Then the main verb comes last in its *-ing* form, showing the action
in motion: *I have been sitting here for three hours.* The main verb *sit* never changes
its ending; the tool verbs in front of it carry all the time information.

**5. How native speakers see it.** *A useful mental model*: in lesson 04, the past
continuous was a film running in the background. In lesson 05, the present perfect was
a bridge from the past to now. The present perfect continuous is **the film playing on
the bridge**: a moving clip that runs from the past right up to your feet. This is a way
to picture it, not a proven fact about how native speakers think.

Esta lección depende de la **idea clave 4: el inglés marca tiempo y finalización con

**1. El panorama general.** El inglés no cambia la terminación del verbo principal
para mostrar tiempo o finalización; conecta verbos herramienta, y cada uno hace un solo
trabajo. *Have* dice "esto llega hasta ahora". *Be + -ing* dice "esto está en

**2. De dónde viene.** La parte *be + -ing* probablemente nació de dos patrones poco
comunes del inglés antiguo y del inglés medio temprano: *be* + un participio presente
terminado en *-ende*, y *be* + una preposición + un sustantivo terminado en *-unge*.

terminación, *-ing*; después de eso, el progresivo se volvió mucho más común, y llegó a

exactamente la forma apilada *have been + -ing*, así que aquí no se da una fecha.

**3. El problema que genera.** Ahora el inglés tiene dos herramientas separadas, y
ninguna alcanza sola. *Have* + participio conecta con el ahora, pero muchas veces suena
terminado: *I've written the essay* significa que ya está listo. *Be + -ing* muestra
algo en progreso, pero solo en un momento: *I am sitting here for three hours* no
describe las tres horas que ya pasaron; suena a un plan para las próximas tres horas.
Entonces, ¿cómo dices "esta actividad empezó antes, se ha estado moviendo todo el
tiempo y llega justo hasta ahora"?

**4. La solución del inglés.** Apilar las dos herramientas: **have + been + -ing**.
*Have* va primero y conecta con el ahora. Como *have* siempre pide un participio pasado
después, *be* se convierte en *been*. Luego el verbo principal va de último en su forma
*-ing*, mostrando la acción en movimiento: *I have been sitting here for three hours.*
El verbo principal *sit* nunca cambia su terminación; los verbos herramienta de adelante
cargan toda la información de tiempo.

**5. Cómo lo ven los hablantes nativos.** *Un modelo mental útil*: en la lección 04, el
past continuous era una película corriendo de fondo. En la lección 05, el present

película proyectándose sobre el puente**: un clip en movimiento que corre desde el
pasado hasta tus pies. Es una forma de imaginarlo, no un hecho comprobado sobre cómo
piensan los hablantes nativos.

### Also: how Spanish says it with *llevar* or *hace... que*, and why English can't copy it

Spanish has two everyday ways to say "this started in the past and is still going on":
*llevar* + time + gerund (*Llevo tres años trabajando aquí*) and *hace* + time + *que* +
present (*Hace tres años que trabajo aquí*), plus a third order, present + *desde hace*
(*Trabajo aquí desde hace tres años*). In all three, a separate word carries the time
link: *llevar*, *hace* or *desde hace*. That's why the Spanish main verb can stay in the
simple present (*trabajo*). English has no separate "duration verb" like *llevar*: the
duration has to live inside the verb form itself, and that job belongs to *have*. The
present tense alone can't reach back; English grammars list *"They know each other
since January"* and *"They're knowing each other since January"* as errors for "time up
to now," and *I am sitting here for three hours* sounds like an intention, not a
history. So a word-for-word copy of *hace tres años que trabajo* (*"I work here since
three years"*) breaks twice: wrong tense and wrong preposition. The same logic continues
into the past: Spanish *llevaba dos horas durmiendo* becomes *I had been sleeping for
two hours* (a later lesson).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

El español tiene dos formas cotidianas de decir "esto empezó en el pasado y sigue":
*llevar* + tiempo + gerundio (*Llevo tres años trabajando aquí*) y *hace* + tiempo +
*que* + presente (*Hace tres años que trabajo aquí*), más un tercer orden, presente +
*desde hace* (*Trabajo aquí desde hace tres años*). En las tres, una palabra aparte carga
el vínculo con el tiempo: *llevar*, *hace* o *desde hace*. Por eso el verbo principal en
español puede quedarse en presente simple (*trabajo*). El inglés no tiene un "verbo de
duración" aparte como *llevar*: la duración tiene que vivir dentro de la forma verbal, y
ese trabajo le toca a *have*. El presente solo no alcanza a mirar hacia atrás; las
gramáticas de inglés marcan *"They know each other since January"* y *"They're knowing
each other since January"* como errores para "tiempo hasta ahora", y *I am sitting here
for three hours* suena a una intención, no a una historia. Por eso copiar palabra por
palabra *hace tres años que trabajo* (*"I work here since three years"*) falla dos
veces: tiempo verbal equivocado y preposición equivocada. La misma lógica sigue en el
pasado: *llevaba dos horas durmiendo* se vuelve *I had been sleeping for two hours* (una

### Also: why *I've been painting the room* and *I've painted the room* mean different things

Both forms connect to now, but they point the camera at different things. The present
perfect simple emphasises **completion**: *I've painted the room* means the job is
done; come and look at the walls. The present perfect continuous emphasises **the
activity**: *I've been painting the room* means that's what you've been doing; maybe
it's finished, maybe not, and maybe the evidence is the paint on your hands. That's also
why numbers push you to the simple form: *I've painted two rooms* counts finished
results, and a count only makes sense for things that are complete. And verbs that
happen at a single point (*start*, *stop*, *finish*) normally don't take the continuous
here at all: *Has the concert started?*, not *"Has the concert been starting?"*

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

Las dos formas conectan con el ahora, pero apuntan la cámara a cosas distintas. El
present perfect simple enfatiza **que está terminado**: *I've painted the room*
significa que el trabajo está listo; ven a ver las paredes. El present perfect
continuous enfatiza **la actividad**: *I've been painting the room* significa que eso es
lo que has estado haciendo; quizás terminaste, quizás no, y quizás la evidencia es la
pintura en tus manos. Por eso los números te empujan a la forma simple: *I've painted
two rooms* cuenta resultados terminados, y contar solo tiene sentido con cosas
completas. Y los verbos que pasan en un solo punto (*start*, *stop*, *finish*)
normalmente no usan el continuo aquí: *Has the concert started?*, no *"Has the concert
been starting?"*

</details>

### Also: why state verbs still refuse *-ing* here

The *-ing* part of this tense comes from *be + -ing*, and that part brings its own rule
with it. State verbs describe situations that stay the same the whole time they last
(*know*, *like*, *believe*, *own*), and in English they normally don't take the
progressive: *"I am knowing the answer"* is wrong (you saw this in lesson 04 with *"I
was knowing"*). Stacking *have* in front doesn't change that. So for states, English
uses the plain present perfect even when the situation is still going on: *I've known
her for years*, *I've been here for half an hour* (never *"I've been being here"*). In
spoken English you will sometimes hear sense verbs in the continuous for something
temporary (*I've been hearing strange noises outside*), but *know*, *like* and
*believe* stay simple. Interestingly, Spanish shows a similar limit: a Spanish grammar
guide notes that states like *saber*, *tener* and *ser* generally don't fit *llevar* +
gerund either, and Spanish prefers *desde hace* for them. *A useful mental model* for
the English rule: *-ing* films something moving; a state has no moving parts to film.

<summary>Ver en español</summary>

La parte *-ing* de este tiempo viene de *be + -ing*, y esa parte trae su propia regla.
Los verbos de estado describen situaciones que se mantienen iguales todo el tiempo que
duran (*know*, *like*, *believe*, *own*), y en inglés normalmente no usan el progresivo:
*"I am knowing the answer"* está mal (lo viste en la lección 04 con *"I was knowing"*).
Poner *have* adelante no cambia eso. Entonces, para los estados, el inglés usa el
present perfect simple aunque la situación siga: *I've known her for years*, *I've been
here for half an hour* (nunca *"I've been being here"*). En el inglés hablado a veces
vas a oír verbos de los sentidos en continuo para algo temporal (*I've been hearing
strange noises outside*), pero *know*, *like* y *believe* se quedan en simple. Algo

regla del inglés: *-ing* filma algo que se mueve; un estado no tiene partes que se
muevan para filmar.

</details>

## 6. When NOT to use it (traps)

- ❌ *"I am working here since three years."* → ✅ *"I've been working here for three
  years."*
  *Why:* two problems. The present continuous can't reach back over "time up to now"
  (it sounds like a plan), so you need *have been*. And *since* needs a starting point
  (*since 2023*); a length of time takes *for*.
- ❌ *"I've been knowing her for years."* → ✅ *"I've known her for years."*
  *Why:* *know* is a state verb. States refuse *-ing*, even inside this tense, so English
  uses the present perfect simple.
- ❌ *"How long are you living here?"* → ✅ *"How long have you been living here?"*
  *Why:* *how long* up to now needs *have*. *"How long are you living here?"* sounds
  like a question about your plans (how long you will stay), not about the time you've
  already spent there.
- ❌ *"I've been writing three emails this morning."* → ✅ *"I've written three emails
  this morning."*
  *Why:* *three emails* counts finished results, and counts go with the simple form.
  The continuous works without the number: *I've been writing emails all morning.*

<details>
<summary>Ver en español (por qué de cada trampa)</summary>

1. Dos problemas. El presente continuo no alcanza a mirar el "tiempo hasta ahora"
   (suena a un plan), así que necesitas *have been*. Y *since* necesita un punto de
   inicio (*since 2023*); una cantidad de tiempo va con *for*.
2. *Know* es un verbo de estado. Los estados rechazan *-ing*, incluso dentro de este
   tiempo, así que el inglés usa el present perfect simple.
3. *How long* hasta ahora necesita *have*. *"How long are you living here?"* suena a
   una pregunta sobre tus planes (cuánto tiempo te vas a quedar), no sobre el tiempo
   que ya llevas ahí.
4. *Three emails* cuenta resultados terminados, y los conteos van con la forma simple.
   El continuo funciona sin el número: *I've been writing emails all morning.*
</details>

## 7. Hack

**Clock or counter?** Before you speak, ask what you're looking at. If you're looking
at a **clock** (how long, still moving, the activity itself): *have been + -ing*. If
you're looking at a **counter** (how many, done, the result): *have* + past participle.
And a state (*know*, *like*, *believe*) has no clock that ticks: it always takes the
simple form.

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

**¿Reloj o contador?** Antes de hablar, pregúntate qué estás mirando. Si miras un
**reloj** (cuánto tiempo, todavía en movimiento, la actividad en sí): *have been +
-ing*. Si miras un **contador** (cuántos, terminado, el resultado): *have* + participio
pasado. Y un estado (*know*, *like*, *believe*) no tiene un reloj que avance: siempre
va con la forma simple.
</details>

*Theme: art and the psychology of attention (a short fictional first-person story).*

I **have been painting** the same window for three months. It is the small window above
my kitchen sink, and I **have been standing** in front of it almost every morning since
June. Friends keep asking how many paintings I **have finished**. The honest answer is
two, and I **have thrown** both of them away.

This probably sounds like failure, but something else **has been happening**. I **have
been looking** closely at things I never saw before: the light turns green around seven,
the glass holds a faint reflection of the street, and a spider **has been rebuilding**
its web in the same corner every night. The window **hasn't changed**. My attention
**has**.

Before this project, I believed art was about the finished object. I **have known**
painters who work that way, and their results are beautiful. But the activity **has been
teaching** me something the results never could: seeing is a skill, and it grows in the
doing, not in the product you hang on a wall.

## 10. Summary

1. Use *have been + -ing* for an activity that started in the past and runs up to now,
   or that just stopped and left evidence: *I've been waiting for two hours.*
2. Build it with two tool verbs stacked (*have* + *been*) and the main verb in *-ing*;
   use *for* + length, *since* + starting point.
3. Biggest trap: the present can't reach back. Not *"I am working here since three
   years"* but *"I've been working here for three years"*; and states or counts take
   the simple form (*I've known her*, *I've written three emails*).

<details>
<summary>Ver en español</summary>

1. Usa *have been + -ing* para una actividad que empezó en el pasado y llega hasta
   ahora, o que acaba de parar y dejó evidencia: *I've been waiting for two hours.*
2. Se arma con dos verbos herramienta apilados (*have* + *been*) y el verbo principal
   en *-ing*; usa *for* + cantidad de tiempo, *since* + punto de inicio.
3. La trampa más grande: el presente no alcanza a mirar atrás. No *"I am working here
   since three years"* sino *"I've been working here for three years"*; y los estados o
   los conteos van con la forma simple (*I've known her*, *I've written three emails*).
</details>

## Sources for "Why it works this way"

Big Idea conectada en la sección 5: idea 4 de `docs/ideas-clave.md` (verbos
herramienta). Lecciones conectadas: 04 (past continuous) y 05 (present perfect simple).

Todas las URLs de abajo fueron buscadas y leídas (fetch exitoso) en esta sesión,
2026-09-26:

- [Uses of English verb forms, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uses_of_English_verb_forms):
  describe el present perfect progressive como *have/has* + *been* (participio de *be*)
  + participio presente; explica que *I am sitting here for three hours* implica una
  intención; y que con verbos de estado se usa el perfecto simple (*I've been here for
  half an hour*, no *"I've been being here"*).
- [Continuous and progressive aspects, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_and_progressive_aspects):
  origen del progresivo inglés en *beon/wesan* + participio en *-ende* y en *be* +
  preposición + gerundio en *-unge*, la fusión en *-ing* durante el inglés medio y la
  forma actual hacia el siglo XVIII.
- [Archived peer-reviewed paper on the history of BE + Ving, University of Antwerp repository](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/91f76f/144377.pdf):
  explica que hasta el inglés antiguo *be + -ing* expresaba sobre todo un estado
  (temporal) y que la función progresiva se desarrolló después.
- [Stative verb, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stative_verb): define verbo
  de estado vs. dinámico y muestra que en inglés los estados no suelen ir en
  progresivo (*"I am knowing the answer"*).
- [Present perfect continuous, Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/present-perfect-continuous-i-have-been-working):
  forma, actividad reciente con resultado visible, actividad que sigue, repetida, y
  preguntas con *How long*.
- [Present perfect simple or present perfect continuous?, Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/present-perfect-simple-or-present-perfect-continuous):
  simple = evento completado, continuo = actividad en curso; verbos de un solo punto
  (*start*, *finish*) no van en continuo; verbos mentales y de los sentidos no van en
  continuo (*"haven't been knowing"*), con la excepción hablada de *I've been hearing*.
- [Present perfect: typical errors, Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/present-perfect-typical-errors):
  *since* no va con cantidades de tiempo; el presente simple o continuo no se usa para
  "tiempo hasta ahora" (*"They're knowing each other since January"*).
- [Present perfect, British Council LearnEnglish](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-perfect):
  el present perfect continuous enfatiza que algo sigue en el presente, y no se usa con
  verbos de estado (*I've always liked John*).
- [Llevar + tiempo + gerundio, Elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar/spanish-spain/verbs/periphrastic/llevar-gerund):
  *llevar* + tiempo + gerundio, *hace* + tiempo + *que* + presente y *desde hace*; el
  inglés no tiene un "verbo de duración" aparte; los estados (*saber*, *tener*, *ser*)
  en general no encajan en *llevar* + gerundio.
- [Using llevar + present participle, Kwiziq Spanish](https://spanish.kwiziq.com/revision/grammar/using-llevar-gerund-to-express-duration-of-an-action):
  *llevar* + gerundio se usa en presente e imperfecto, y sus equivalentes en inglés son
  los tiempos perfectos o perfectos progresivos (*have been doing*, *had been doing*).
